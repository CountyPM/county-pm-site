On May 3, 2022, the Oxnard City Council held a meeting. By the end of it, they had enacted two sweeping ordinances — one capping annual rent increases at 4%, the other establishing just cause eviction protections and mandatory relocation assistance — that fundamentally altered the property rights of every residential landlord in the city. No ballot measure. No public vote. No opportunity for property owners or citizens to challenge the ordinances through referendum before they took effect. Just five council members and a procedural tool called an urgency ordinance.

Three times — in 2018, 2020, and 2024 — the California electorate had been offered the chance to expand local rent control authority. Three times they said no, each time by roughly 20 percentage points. Proposition 10 in 2018 failed 59% to 41%. Proposition 21 in 2020 failed 60% to 40%. Proposition 33 in 2024 failed by a similar margin. The message from California voters was consistent, clear, and decisive: we do not want this.

The Oxnard City Council enacted it anyway.

> "Three times California voters said no to rent control expansion — each time by 20 points. The Oxnard City Council called an emergency and did it in one meeting."

## The Mechanism: What Is an Urgency Ordinance?

In California, a city council can bypass the standard 30-day waiting period before a new ordinance takes effect by declaring it an urgency measure. Under Government Code § 36937, urgency ordinances require a four-fifths supermajority of the council and a finding that the measure is "necessary for the immediate preservation of the public peace, health, or safety." They take effect immediately upon adoption.

The mechanism exists for genuine emergencies — a bridge collapses, a contamination event threatens public health, an immediate public safety threat requires immediate response. It is a tool designed for situations where waiting 30 days would itself cause harm.

What constitutes an emergency in Oxnard in May 2022? Housing costs had been rising in Ventura County for years. Displacement pressures on low-income renters were real and well-documented — but they were not new in May 2022. There was no sudden event, no acute crisis that distinguished that particular month from the twelve months prior. The housing affordability challenges that motivated the ordinance had been building for a decade.

The urgency designation is not incidental. It is the mechanism by which the council foreclosed the possibility of a citizen referendum. Under California law, ordinances passed by a city council — unless they are urgency measures — can be referred to the voters through a referendum petition. An urgency ordinance cannot be referred. The designation does not just accelerate the timeline. It eliminates an entire avenue of democratic challenge.

> "An urgency ordinance cannot be challenged by referendum. The 'emergency' designation didn't just speed up the process — it shut the door on public opposition entirely."

## The Constitutional Question: The Property Rights Question

Private property rights in California are protected under both the state constitution and the federal Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause, which prohibits government from taking private property for public use without just compensation. The legal question rent control ordinances perpetually raise — and perpetually navigate around — is whether restricting a property owner's ability to charge market rent, choose their tenants, or exit the rental market constitutes a regulatory taking requiring compensation.

Courts have generally allowed rent control to survive takings challenges so long as owners retain a "fair return" on their investment. Oxnard's ordinance includes a fair return petition process — a mechanism by which landlords can apply to exceed the 4% cap if they can demonstrate the cap denies them a reasonable return. The existence of that process is the legal shield against a takings claim.

But the fair return petition process deserves scrutiny. The cost and burden of filing a petition falls entirely on the property owner. The standard is vague. The city's rent office — funded by fees charged to property owners — adjudicates the petition. It is not a neutral process. For a small landlord managing a duplex or a four-unit building, the practical reality is that the petition process is too expensive and too uncertain to be a meaningful remedy. The nominal right exists. The practical right does not.

More fundamentally: the question is not only whether the ordinance survives a legal challenge, but whether it is a legitimate exercise of police power or an appropriation of private economic value for a public benefit that the public — three times — declined to authorize at the ballot box.

> "The council made the finding, adopted the ordinance, and immunized it from challenge — all in the same meeting. The voters had no procedural recourse before it was law."

## The Broader Question: Legality Is Not the Same as Legitimacy

None of this is to argue that Oxnard's tenants face no real hardship. They do. Ventura County's rental market is expensive, and displacement from communities where people have built lives is a genuine harm. These concerns are not manufactured — they are real, and they deserve serious policy responses.

The argument here is narrower: the method of enactment matters. A policy imposed without democratic legitimacy — over the expressed objection of the electorate, using a procedural tool designed for genuine emergencies — carries a structural deficit that legitimate policy does not. It breeds resentment where cooperation might exist. It forecloses compromise. And it produces law that the people most affected by it — property owners and tenants alike — never had a meaningful opportunity to shape.

The Oxnard City Council acted legally, through mechanisms California law permits. But legality and legitimacy are not the same thing. And in a democratic system, the gap between them is worth examining carefully — especially when the stakes include the property rights of citizens who never got to vote.
