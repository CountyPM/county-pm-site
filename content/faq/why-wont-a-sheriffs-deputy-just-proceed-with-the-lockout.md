Because removing the wrong household from its home is a serious legal exposure for the deputy and the county, and a mismatched address is exactly the kind of doubt that makes them stop. The writ of possession authorizes the sheriff to remove the occupants of one specific, identified property — nothing broader. If the number on the curb doesn't match the number on the writ, the deputy has no reliable way, in the field, to confirm this is that property.

The deputy isn't equipped or expected to investigate a tenant's claim that the address changed, adjudicate a dispute, or take the landlord's word over what's physically posted on the building. Their job at that moment is narrow: match the paperwork to the premises. When that match fails, the institutionally cautious choice — for the deputy and for the county's liability — is to decline and let the landlord come back with proof.

That proof standard exists for a reason unrelated to any one bad-faith tenant: sheriffs enforce writs against the wrong unit often enough, in multi-unit buildings and unposted rurals, that "verify the address before you remove someone" is the correct default, not an overreaction to one scam.

This is general information, not legal advice; how a specific sheriff's civil unit handles a disputed address can vary, so confirm your county's procedure with a qualified professional.
