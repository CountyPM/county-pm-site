Yes. The January 1, 2030 sunset is written into the statute itself: Civil Code §1947.12 remains in effect only until that date and is repealed as of it, and the just-cause provisions in §1946.2 carry the same date. On or before January 1, 2030, the Legislative Analyst's Office owes the Legislature a report on whether the Act was effective. Nothing happens automatically except expiration — the Legislature can extend it, amend it, or let it lapse.

Do not plan around the lapse. Expiration is the default, not the forecast. The realistic outcomes are an extension, a narrowed or lowered version, or replacement by something else, and all three require ordinary legislation rather than a ballot measure. What an owner actually gets from the date is roughly three and a half years of knowable rules — which is the most useful thing on the list, and it is wasting.

Amendment is the live risk, and it is cheaper than repeal. AB 1157 (Kalra) would have cut the cap to the lower of 2% plus CPI or 5%, removed the separately-alienable single-family exemption, and deleted the 2030 sunset so the framework became permanent. It failed in the Assembly Judiciary Committee in January 2026 on a 4-3 vote with five members not voting, short of the seven required — but it was carried as a two-year measure, so the drafted language exists and can return. Note the sequence in that hearing: the author offered to strike the single-family exemption removal to buy votes before the bill died on the rent cap. The exemption is the bargaining chip, and everyone in Sacramento knows it.

Repeal from the other direction looks unlikely. Three statewide ballot attempts to expand rent control — Proposition 10 in 2018, Proposition 21 in 2020, and Proposition 33 in 2024 — all failed at close to the same margin, roughly 59% to 60% against, against a persistent and well-funded sponsor. The 2024 campaign cost both sides a combined $175 million and moved the number by less than a point. That is why the practical risk sits in Sacramento rather than at the ballot box: a change can pass in a budget trailer bill, in June, with two weeks of notice.

For a Ventura County owner, the operating conclusion is narrow. Underwrite the carry rather than the policy, price the single-family exemption into any decision to add units as a real line item rather than a footnote, and if you are deciding whether to recapitalize, do it while the underwriting inputs are still legible.

This is general information, not legal advice. Statutory sunsets and pending bills change, so confirm the current text of §1947.12 and §1946.2 and the status of any pending measure before relying on the date.
