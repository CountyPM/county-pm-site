Sometimes — but the first question is not how much equity is there, it is what you are currently carrying. Pulling equity can make sense when the borrowed money is redeployed into something that earns more than the after-tax cost of the new debt. It stops making sense when the funds pay for consumption or sit idle. Negative cash flow on a leveraged rental with nothing productive on the other side of the loan is a warning sign, not a strategy.

Run two tests, not one. The first is the obvious one: does the after-tax return on the redeployed capital clear the after-tax cost of the borrowing? The second is the one owners skip: what does the *method* of the draw cost you? A cash-out refinance reprices the entire first mortgage at today's rate. On a $600,000 balance moving from 4% to 6.75%, that is roughly $13,000 a year of cost you incur before the redeployed capital earns a dollar — and it is not a cost of the new money, it is a cost of how you took it. In a worked Ventura County example, refinancing a 3.5% note of about $460,000 into a $712,500 loan near 7% to pull roughly $240,000 turned an $850-a-month-positive property into about $1,465 a month negative, roughly $17,600 a year. That is not unlocking equity. That is selling the low-rate note that was the asset.

If you hold a low first, take the money in second position and leave it alone. A home equity loan, a HELOC, an FHA Title I Property Improvement Loan (government-insured and designed to sit subordinate), or a renovation second underwritten on after-renovation value all touch only the equity you need. None of them are free, and they should still clear the breakeven — but they let you run the arithmetic on the incremental debt rather than on a whole new blended loan. For an owner already at or near market rate the calculus flips, and a single clean refinance can be the simpler structure.

The strongest answer to "redeployed into what?" is often the parcel itself. For an owner with a low Proposition 13 basis, converting a downstairs bedroom into a junior unit and a detached garage into a second unit reassesses the new construction only — the original assessed value does not move — so income goes up without repricing the whole tax bill the way a buyer of the same property would face. On illustrative Ventura County numbers, roughly $230,000 of conversion spend adding about $23,800 of annual net operating income is a marginal return near 10.3%, against stabilized apartment product trading near 5.4%. That clears the borrowing-cost test more comfortably than most alternatives an owner gets shown.

Two costs belong in that same model rather than in the surprise column: the AB 1482 single-family rent-cap exemption generally goes when the deed restriction barring separate sale of a junior unit records, and a three- or four-unit property sells to a narrower buyer pool than the house it used to be. Figures here are illustrative and will differ from your property. For Ventura County owners with a low-rate first, we walk the breakeven — and the choice of instrument — before recommending any equity pull, because the math, not the size of the equity, decides whether it is worth it.
