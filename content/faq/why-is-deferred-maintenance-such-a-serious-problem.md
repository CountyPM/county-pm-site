Because it compounds quietly, and by the time it is obvious the cheap fixes are gone. Deferred maintenance is not a series of separate small decisions — it is one slow loop that tightens on itself.

The loop runs like this. A postponed repair becomes a larger repair, because water and wear do not wait. The property shows worse, so better-qualified tenants pass on it and you rent to whoever is left. Turnover and vacancy rise. Rents soften relative to the neighborhood, because you are now competing on price rather than condition. Thinner cash flow makes the next deferral easier to justify — and it is genuinely harder to say yes to a repair when the property is producing less.

The end state is a property that needs more work than the owner can fund. At that point the options narrow to a discounted sale, and the buyer will price in every deferred dollar and then some, because they are underwriting uncertainty about what else was ignored. Deferred maintenance sells at a discount larger than its cost to fix.

The practical defense is unglamorous: fund the reserve before you take distributions, address items when they are small, and treat the annual condition walk as a financial exercise rather than a cosmetic one. Money spent on a property that is holding well is the cheapest money in the whole strategy.
