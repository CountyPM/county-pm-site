Yes. Since Senate Bill 567 took effect on April 1, 2024, every city attorney and county counsel in California has express authority to enforce the Tenant Protection Act (Civil Code §1947.12's rent cap and §1946.2's just-cause rules) directly, including seeking court injunctions against violations within their own jurisdiction. The state Attorney General holds the same enforcement power statewide, and SB 567 also added protections against landlords falsely claiming a just-cause basis to evict.

This closed what had been a real gap: before SB 567, enforcement leaned almost entirely on an individual tenant filing their own lawsuit. A city attorney's office has investigators, subpoena-adjacent tools, and no need for a single injured tenant willing to sue — which is why several California cities have opened active TPA enforcement efforts against landlords since 2024, with settlements requiring restitution, monetary penalties, and years of compliance monitoring.

The practical shift for owners is that "no tenant has complained" is no longer the relevant risk gauge. A pattern of rent increases across multiple units, or a habit of citing just cause loosely, can now draw attention from a city attorney's office directly, independent of any tenant taking action.

This is general information, not legal advice; enforcement posture varies by city and county, so confirm your local exposure with a qualified professional.
