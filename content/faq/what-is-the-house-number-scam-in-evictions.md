It's a last-minute stalling tactic: a tenant facing a sheriff's lockout removes or swaps the house numbers on the curb or the building, so the address in front of the deputy no longer matches the address on the writ of possession. The deputy has no way to confirm, on the spot, that the property they're standing at is the one the court ordered them to clear — so the safe move for them is to walk away and make the landlord reschedule.

It works because the writ identifies the property by street address, not by GPS coordinates or a photograph. A deputy enforcing a lockout is doing a narrow, procedural job: match the address on the paperwork to the address on the building. Take away that match and there's nothing left for them to verify against, no matter how obviously correct the landlord is standing right there.

The tactic doesn't beat the eviction — the judgment still stands and the writ is still valid. What it buys the tenant is time: days to weeks while the landlord proves the property, gets the sheriff's civil unit comfortable, and reschedules the lockout. For an owner who has already spent months getting to judgment day, that delay is the whole point of the scam.
