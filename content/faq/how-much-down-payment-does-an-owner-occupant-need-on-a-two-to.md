As little as 5% down. Since November 2023, Fannie Mae has allowed 5% down on an owner-occupied two-, three-, or four-unit principal residence, where the buyer intends to live in one of the units. FHA financing can go lower still — 3.5% for a qualifying borrower. An investor buying the identical building needs 25%. On a $950,000 property that is roughly $47,500 versus $237,500, before a dollar of renovation.

That gap is the single largest structural difference between the two buyers, and it decides who can do the deal rather than merely who pays less. The rate difference — roughly half a point to a full point, driven by the agency loan-level price adjustments that apply to non-owner-occupied loans — matters over thirty years. The down payment matters on the day of the offer. For a buyer without a quarter million dollars in liquid capital, owner-occupancy is not the cheaper path to a small multifamily property; it is the only one.

The occupancy requirement is real, not a formality. It is a representation you make to a lender, and the loan documents set the period. On renovation products the timing is explicit: FHA 203(k) generally requires occupancy within 60 days of closing, or 90 if renovation delays move-in, as a genuine primary residence. Mean it when you sign it, and read your own loan documents for the specific term before you plan a move.

Renovation financing is the part most buyers never learn exists. FHA 203(k), Fannie Mae HomeStyle Renovation, and Freddie Mac CHOICERenovation roll the purchase and the renovation into a single thirty-year mortgage underwritten on the after-improved value — you submit plans and contractor bids before escrow closes, and the appraiser values the property as it will exist when the work is done. For a strategy whose central problem is that construction cash goes out before the value arrives, that is the missing piece. The tradeoffs: scope approved in advance, draws inspected, changes requiring lender sign-off, FHA county loan limits that scale by unit count, an FHA cap at the lesser of purchase price plus renovation cost or 110% of after-improved value, and on a 203(k) no contractor you are related to or in business with.

Two things not to assume. Reserve requirements are program-specific and are not automatically light on the owner-occupant side — the low-down two-to-four unit products carry substantial reserve requirements of their own, so get the actual number from your lender. And budget contingency on any renovation: 10% to 20%, then another 10%, because renovation budgets on older houses are wrong in one direction only.

This is general information, not lending advice. Agency guidelines, county loan limits, and pricing change frequently, and eligibility depends on your credit, income, reserves, and the specific property — confirm current terms with a licensed mortgage professional before relying on any figure here.
