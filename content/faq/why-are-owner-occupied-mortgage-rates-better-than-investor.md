Lenders price loans by risk, and a borrower who lives in the home is treated as lower risk than one who does not. If money gets tight, people prioritize the mortgage on the roof over their own heads ahead of a loan on a property they merely own, so loans on principal residences default less often. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac reflect that directly through loan-level price adjustments tied to occupancy: an investment-property loan carries extra pricing on top of an otherwise identical loan, which the borrower feels as a higher rate, more points, or both. Your lender is not marking you up — it is passing through a fee that applies no matter which bank you call.

The rate gap is roughly half a point to a full point for the same borrower on the same house. Behind that, the LLPA stack on a non-owner-occupied loan can reach 4.125% of the loan amount at higher leverage, running roughly 2.125 to 3.375 points at 75% loan-to-value with a 740 credit score. On a $712,500 loan, three quarters of a point of rate is about $350 a month, and over the life of the loan it runs well into six figures.

The down payment gap is wider than the rate gap, and on small multifamily it is what actually decides who can buy. An owner-occupant can use FHA financing with as little as 3.5% down on a home to live in. On a two-to-four unit property — which is where accessory dwelling unit and conversion strategies land — Fannie Mae has allowed 5% down for an owner-occupied principal residence since November 2023, while an investor buying the identical building needs 25%. On a $950,000 property that is $47,500 versus $237,500, before a dollar of construction. That single line decides whether the deal is possible at all, not merely whether it is cheaper.

Reserves and underwriting move the same direction, but the details are program-specific. Investor financing generally requires more months of reserves on the subject property plus additional months for each other financed property, held in the borrower's own accounts, and it leans more heavily on the property than on the borrower. Owner-occupant reserve requirements are not automatically lighter on every program — the 5%-down two-to-four unit product carries substantial reserve requirements of its own — so confirm the actual figure with your lender rather than assuming.

This is the reasoning behind the live-in-first sequence: buy as an owner-occupant, occupy the property genuinely and for the period your loan documents require, and convert it to a rental later. You keep the rate and the low down payment you honestly qualified for, and there is no requirement to refinance into costlier investor terms when the use changes. If you need cash out afterward, a second mortgage that leaves the original first alone is usually cheaper than a full cash-out refinance in this rate environment.

This is general information, not financial advice. Pricing grids, program rules, and county loan limits change frequently, and eligibility turns on your credit, income, reserves, and the specific property — confirm current terms with a licensed mortgage professional before counting on a rate or down payment.
