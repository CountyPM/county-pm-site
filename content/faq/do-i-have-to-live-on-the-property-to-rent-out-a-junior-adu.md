Not necessarily, and this changed recently. AB 1154, approved October 10, 2025, narrowed the owner-occupancy requirement for junior accessory dwelling units so that a local ordinance may impose it only where the junior unit shares sanitation facilities with the main house. Give the junior unit its own bathroom and, as a general matter, no owner-occupancy requirement attaches — which means an absentee owner can rent both the main house and the junior unit.

That single detail decides whether the project works. A downstairs bedroom that already has its own full bath is the most valuable feature on a conversion candidate, because the plumbing is stubbed on that side of the house and because the separate bath is what removes the occupancy condition. Share the bath with the main house and you must live on the property; give it its own and you do not. The law also lets the owner satisfy any surviving occupancy requirement by living in either the main home or the junior unit, and it does not apply to government agencies, land trusts, or qualifying housing organizations.

Two limits worth knowing before you plan around it. A junior unit must be rented for a term longer than 30 days, so it is not a short-term rental strategy. And local ordinances have lagged the statute — city ADU pages and handouts in Ventura County still describe the old owner-occupancy rule in places, so expect to cite the current state law at the counter rather than assume the published handout is right.

The occupancy question is separate from the rent-cap question, and the rent-cap question is the more expensive one. State law requires a recorded deed restriction prohibiting separate sale of a junior unit, running with the land. Once that restriction records, the parcel generally stops being alienable separate from the title to any other dwelling unit — which is the exact test AB 1482 uses for the single-family exemption. So the same conversion that frees you from living there can pull the main house under the statewide rent cap. Price that trade before you break ground.

This is general information, not legal advice. Accessory dwelling unit law has changed in each of the last several legislative sessions and local implementation varies, so confirm the current state requirement and your city's ordinance with your planning department or counsel before you rely on this for a specific parcel.
