A letter from a reliable third party — and California defines that narrowly. Under AB 468 (effective January 2022), the provider issuing an emotional support animal letter must hold a valid, active California license, must have established a client relationship at least 30 days before writing the letter, and must have completed a clinical evaluation of the person. Under California's housing regulations, an online certificate purchased without an individualized assessment from a medical professional is presumptively not reliable third-party information.

That standard is your legitimate screen against the certification mills. The $59 instant certificate with a registration number and a PDF badge was already weak in California before any federal policy changed — you may ask whether the provider is California-licensed, whether there was an actual clinical relationship, and whether the letter reflects an assessment of this person rather than a template with a name dropped in.

What you may not do: demand the diagnosis or medical records, require a specific proprietary form, or ask for a registration number, ID card, vest, or certificate. And if the disability and the disability-related need are apparent, you may not ask for documentation at all — asking anyway is its own violation.

When a letter falls short of the standard, the right move is a cure request, not a denial: state in writing what the documentation is missing and give the tenant a reasonable window (10 to 14 days is the common practitioner standard) to supply compliant paperwork. Most mill letters die there without anyone denying anything — and if compliant documentation comes back, approval was the right outcome anyway.

This is general information, not legal advice; fair-housing rules carry real liability, so confirm your documentation requests against the Civil Rights Department guidance above or with counsel.
