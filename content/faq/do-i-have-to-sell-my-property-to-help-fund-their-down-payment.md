No. You can reach the equity without selling, and there are three ways to do it rather than one — which matters, because the obvious one is often the most expensive.

Selling converts a compounding asset into a one-time pile of cash and triggers a taxable event on the way. Borrowing does neither: loan proceeds are not taxable income to you, and you have not recognized a gain because you have not sold anything. Your original position keeps amortizing while a second clock starts for your child. The question is which instrument gets you there.

## Fund it from somewhere other than the property

The path that gets skipped in both directions. Savings, a bonus, the sale of a different asset, or simply giving less all leave the note and the assessed base completely untouched. Borrowing against the property has a price that shows up every month for years, and the comparison worth running is against helping with a smaller amount — not only against selling.

## If you borrow, look at the first mortgage before you look at the rate

A cash-out refinance replaces cheap money with today's money on the *entire* balance. If your first mortgage carries a rate from the low-rate years, that single decision can move a property from comfortably positive to badly negative — and it resets the amortization schedule back toward the front, where interest consumes most of the payment, surrendering whatever position on the curve the loan had already earned.

A second position — a fixed home equity loan or a HELOC — leaves the first exactly where it is and borrows only against the equity, only for the amount actually needed. The rate is higher and it is charged on a much smaller balance, so the blended cost of borrowing stays far below a full refinance. It can also be paid down aggressively out of income. Rule of thumb: if the first is under roughly four and a half percent, the default is to borrow behind it rather than through it.

Match the instrument to the need. A known lump sum where payment certainty matters points to the fixed second. A HELOC is an adjustable loan wearing different clothes — it moves with prime, and "I'll take it now and it will get cheaper" is a rate forecast, not a plan.

## The test that decides whether the borrowing was a mistake

One question: did the money buy another asset, or did it get spent?

Equity out to fund a down payment starts a second clock — two positions amortizing, two payments a tenant or the market can help carry. Equity out to fund consumption stops the first and buys nothing that keeps running. The transaction looks identical at the closing table and the outcome ten years later is not remotely the same. Helping a child buy sits squarely on the right side of that line, which is exactly why the discipline is worth stating out loud when the proceeds get sized — the temptation is to round the number up while the paperwork is open.

## Run it against a bad year, not a good one

Whatever instrument you use, the payment goes up and the margin gets thinner. Even a modest second at today's rates can consume a property's entire cash-flow cushion and then some. If the higher payment only works when everything cooperates, the loan is too large. Size it against a year with a vacancy and a repair in it.

This is general information, not tax or financial advice; confirm the structure with your CPA before money moves.
