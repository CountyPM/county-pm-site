Talk to them before you file anything. Most nonpayment comes from a real income interruption rather than bad faith, and for roughly the first thirty days those two situations look identical from the outside. The conversation is the cheapest diagnostic you have, and it costs you nothing you can't recover.

A payment plan, a partial arrangement, a referral to local rental assistance, or a negotiated move-out are all faster and less expensive than an unlawful detainer — and they work precisely in the case where the tenant is a decent person having a hard year. That is the case you want to identify early, because it is the one where the legal process is the worst available tool.

Be deliberate about the mechanics, though, because California's notice and payment rules have consequences that are easy to trip. Code of Civil Procedure § 1161 governs the three-day notice to pay rent or quit, and both the content of the notice and how you handle payments after serving it can affect whether the notice survives in court. Accepting a partial payment at the wrong moment, or writing a notice with the wrong amount, can require you to start over. Put anything you agree to in writing, and get advice before you improvise on the notice itself.

If the conversation does not produce a workable arrangement, move promptly. Delay is the expensive choice in a nonpayment case — the arrears grow, the tenant's ability to catch up shrinks, and the eventual outcome is the same one that was available in month one.

This is general information, not legal advice; confirm your situation with a qualified professional.
