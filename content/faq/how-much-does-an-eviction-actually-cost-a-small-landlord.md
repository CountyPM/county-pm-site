Far more than the unpaid rent, and the rent is usually the smallest line on the list. The realistic exposure is several months of no income while the case moves through the court, plus attorney fees, plus a full turnover afterward, plus a money judgment that in most cases is never collected.

The reason it runs long is structural rather than unlucky. An unlawful detainer starts with a statutory notice under Code of Civil Procedure § 1161 that has to be correct on its face — a defect in the notice sends you back to the beginning. If the tenant responds, the case becomes a contested civil matter with a hearing date set by the court's calendar, not yours. The California Courts self-help materials walk through each stage, and reading them in order is the fastest way to see where the months actually go.

Add the costs that arrive after the judgment. A property that has been occupied by someone who could not afford to stay usually needs more than a normal turn, and the vacancy that follows is a full re-lease cycle rather than a scheduled one. Then there is the judgment itself: winning gives you a piece of paper, and collecting on it against a tenant with no income is a separate effort that most small owners never pursue.

For an owner with one rental, that combination is a complete interruption of the property's income — which is why an eviction is the event reserves should be sized against, not a normal vacancy. If your cushion covers sixty vacant days, it does not cover this.

This is general information, not legal advice; confirm your situation with a qualified professional.
