Yes, if the animal is truly a pet. Pet owners are not a protected class, and California does not require you to allow pets. What you can't do is treat a service animal or emotional support animal as a pet: those are assistance animals under fair housing law, and you have to consider them as a reasonable accommodation even under a no-pets policy.

Apply the pet policy the same way to every applicant. A policy enforced for one applicant and waived for another invites a discrimination claim, so decide your rules in advance (allowed animals, numbers, size or weight limits, required insurance) and document when a pet was the reason for a decline.

Starting April 1, 2027, the policy has to be in writing and disclosed up front. SB 1296 (Civil Code §1942.7.5) requires every landlord, including one with a complete no-pets rule, to have a written pet policy, give a copy with each rental application, include a spot on the application to acknowledge receipt, and link it in online listings where possible. The law expressly keeps your right to deny or restrict pets based on that written policy, health or safety, property condition, insurance, or business reasons. But if you charge an application fee before disclosing the policy and the applicant drops out or is ineligible because of it, they can ask for the fee back, and you must refund it within seven business days.

Whether you should turn pet owners away is a separate question. In our experience a blanket no-pets rule shrinks the applicant pool, slows the rent-up, and doesn't keep assistance animals out anyway. Screening the person first and then the animal, with pet rent and a strong pet addendum, usually serves an owner better than a flat no.

This is general information, not legal advice; confirm your pet policy with a qualified professional.
