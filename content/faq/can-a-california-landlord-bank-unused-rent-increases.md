No. There is no banking under AB 1482. An increase you were entitled to take in an earlier 12-month window and did not take is forfeited — it is not stored, carried forward, or recoverable later. A landlord who skipped three years cannot take three years' worth now.

The mechanism is in the arithmetic, not in a separate prohibition. Civil Code §1947.12(a)(1) measures the allowable increase against the **lowest** gross rental rate charged at any time during the 12 months before the effective date. Because the base resets to whatever you actually charged rather than to whatever you were allowed to charge, every year you skip compounds: the following year's cap is computed off a number that never moved, and so is the year after that. Four years of restraint does not produce four years of headroom. It produces a permanently lower base.

The practical consequence for a below-market covered unit is that the gap essentially cannot be closed during the tenancy. Not slowly, not in one correction, not at all. The only thing that resets rent to market is a lawful vacancy — and on a covered unit, ending a tenancy after twelve months requires just cause under §1946.2. That is the whole trap: by the time an owner wants the vacancy, they need a reason for it.

So not raising the rent on a covered unit is a decision with a price, and the price is permanent. A modest annual increase that keeps pace is worth considerably more than the goodwill bought by skipping it, and a tenant who receives a small increase on the same schedule every year is not surprised by one. Set that expectation in the first lease — reviewed annually, adjusted by a stated method, noticed on a fixed date — and then do it in the good years too.

If the unit is genuinely exempt, the calculation inverts. A properly exempted single-family home or condominium has no cap and therefore no forfeiture rule, so staying deliberately below market is available as a strategy rather than imposed as a consequence. Confirm the exemption from the executed lease before relying on that, and note that exemption from the cap is not exemption from the §827 notice periods.

This is general information, not legal advice; rent cap rules and exemptions turn on facts specific to your property, so confirm your situation with a qualified professional before you set an increase schedule.
