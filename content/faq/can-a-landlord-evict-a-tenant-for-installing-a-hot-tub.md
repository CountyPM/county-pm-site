Often yes — but only after giving the tenant a chance to fix it, not as a first move. Under the statewide just-cause rules (Civil Code §1946.2), a material breach of the lease is an at-fault reason a landlord can terminate a tenancy, and installing a large unauthorized fixture like a hot tub on a balcony not built to hold one is a material breach in most leases. The standard first step is a three-day notice to perform covenants or quit under Code of Civil Procedure §1161(3), which gives the tenant three days to cure — here, removing the tub and paying for any damage — before the landlord can proceed toward eviction.

That "cure" step matters and it isn't optional. If the tenant removes the hot tub and makes the property whole within the three days, the breach is cured and there's no just cause to terminate over it — the lease clause did its job as a deterrent, not as a set-up for eviction. Only a tenant who refuses to cure, or who created a genuine safety emergency, moves the landlord toward an actual unlawful detainer filing.

The unauthorized-alterations clause discussed elsewhere in this hub is what turns "the tenant did something odd" into "the tenant breached the lease" — without it, a landlord is arguing habitability and reasonableness from a much weaker position.

This is general information, not legal advice; whether a specific installation counts as a material breach, and whether the cure was adequate, depends on your lease language and the facts, so confirm your situation with a qualified California attorney.
