It depends far more on who you are than on what you build. The same garage conversion, on the same street, under the same code, is a strong deal for someone who already owns the land outright and a thin one for someone buying the house in order to do it. Decide which of those two people you are before you price anything, because they are optimizing for different things and the answer flips between them.

Entitlement is no longer the obstacle. California spent the last several sessions pushing hard the other way — ministerial approval, no replacement parking required on a garage conversion, existing setbacks governing. What decides an ADU project now is construction cost against the rent the finished unit will actually command in that specific neighborhood, and how a lender and an appraiser treat the finished product. Get real bids, plural, and add the items that never appear in them: utility connections, site work, permits, and the months of carrying cost while it is under construction.

**If you already own the land, the numbers usually work.** You are spending against an asset you hold rather than qualifying for a purchase, and Proposition 13 does the heavy lifting: converting reassesses the new construction only, so a house carrying a $400,000 assessed value in a $950,000 market sees annual property tax move from roughly $4,200 to $6,600, while a buyer of the same finished property pays around $12,400. That single gap is most of the reason an existing owner's marginal return can land near 10.3% where a buyer's yield on cost sits closer to 4.6%. The existing owner is buying cash flow. What they do not get is much of a depreciation story — on a long-held house, adjusted basis is usually far below market, so there is not much to segregate beyond the conversion spend itself.

**If you are buying the house in order to do it, the honest result is rental parity, not cash flow.** Run a Ventura County deal all the way through — roughly $780,000 in purchase plus $220,000 of conversion, three units — and the two rentals cover enough that the owner's net housing cost lands near what a three-bedroom rents for locally. Controlling a million-dollar asset on about $35,000 down while tenants amortize the loan is a good outcome. It is not the outcome the brochures promise, and moving out at the end of the twelve-month owner-occupancy period turns it a few hundred dollars a month negative. What the buyer gets instead is basis: full cost basis with no depreciation history, so a cost segregation study runs against $800,000 to $900,000 rather than the conversion spend alone. That is worth real money to someone with an ordinary income problem and nothing at all to someone without one.

Four financing filters eliminate most listings before you tour one. An FHA 203(k) renovation loan can fund only an **attached** ADU, so a detached garage is out. Lenders count only about half the projected rent on a proposed unit, capped against qualifying income — the higher figure you will read about applies to a unit that already exists. The county FHA loan limit caps total project cost. And the downstairs bedroom has to sit on an exterior wall, or there is no independent entrance and no junior unit, which turns a three-unit deal into a two-unit one. All four move; confirm them with a lender who actually does renovation lending before you write an offer.

The rent-cap exemption is the line item people leave off the model, and it is the expensive one. The AB 1482 single-family exemption does not run to single-family homes as a category — it runs to property alienable separate from the title to any other dwelling unit. State law requires a recorded deed restriction prohibiting separate sale of a junior unit, running with the land. The day that restriction records, the parcel generally stops being separately alienable and the exemption goes with it, on the main house too. So the real trade is a permanent, unconditional exemption on one unit in exchange for two or three capped units at roughly twice the gross. Most Ventura County parcels still clear that trade comfortably — a downstairs bedroom that already has its own bath is the cheapest junior unit you will ever create, and AB 1154 narrowed the owner-occupancy requirement so it applies only where the junior unit shares sanitation facilities with the main house. But price the exemption as a line item, not a footnote, and confirm with counsel exactly when it drops for your parcel. It is usually earlier than owners assume.

Do not plan on a short-term-rental tax strategy. Under SB 543 and AB 1154, both approved October 10, 2025, an ADU or JADU has to be rented for a term longer than 30 days. That closes the seven-day short-term rental exception that would otherwise take the activity out of passive treatment under IRC §469 — so a large first-year deduction may simply suspend and sit there if your income is high enough that the $25,000 active participation allowance has phased out. On a remodel specifically, ask your CPA about the partial asset disposition election: it writes off the remaining basis of the garage door, roof sections, and wall systems you removed, and it has to be made on a timely filed return for the year of disposition or it is gone permanently.

Figures here are illustrative and will differ from your parcel and your market. This is general information, not legal or tax advice — take the exemption question to a California attorney and the depreciation question to your CPA before the work starts, not after.
