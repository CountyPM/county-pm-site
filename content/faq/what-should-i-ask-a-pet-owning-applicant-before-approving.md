Screen the person first, then the animal. Credit, income, and rental history tell you how an applicant handles obligations, and that includes how they care for a home with a pet in it. Once the applicant qualifies, ask about the animal itself: what kind it is, its age and weight, whether it is spayed or neutered, whether a cat stays indoors, and how a dog was trained.

Those questions track where pet damage actually comes from. Puppies chew and have accidents until they're trained, and older dogs can lose control late in life, so a mature, house-trained adult dog is usually the lowest risk. For cats, the main problem is spraying, which is far more common in cats that aren't neutered.

Get a prior landlord's reference on the animal, not just the tenant. Ask whether there was any damage, any complaints from neighbors, and whether the deposit came back in full. A landlord who would rent to the same pet again is the best evidence you'll get.

Then put the protection in writing. Require renter's insurance with liability coverage, especially for dogs; use a pet addendum that names each animal, prohibits additional ones, and spells out responsibility for flooring, odor, and flea treatment at move-out; and schedule periodic inspections to catch small problems early. If the home is in an HOA, check its pet rules before you approve anything.

None of this applies to a service animal or emotional support animal. Those are not pets, and the questions you may ask about them are much narrower, so handle any assistance-animal request under fair-housing rules rather than your pet screening.
