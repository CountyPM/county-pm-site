No. High rents mainly reflect a shortage of homes, not landlord behavior. When demand grows and policy limits how many homes can be built, prices and rents rise. In Ventura County, voter-approved growth boundaries and slow local approvals have kept supply tight for decades, while the median price of an existing single-family home here was about $937,500 in mid-2026 and the state expects the county and its cities to plan for roughly 24,000 new homes this housing cycle.

Landlords are an easy target because they are visible, but a landlord cannot build a house on land that voters have closed to development. Rents reflect scarcity; they do not create it.

None of this is an argument that growth limits are wrong. Protecting farmland and open space is a real benefit that residents value, but every policy has a cost, and this one falls on supply. The honest question to ask about housing costs is how many homes were allowed to be built, and where.
