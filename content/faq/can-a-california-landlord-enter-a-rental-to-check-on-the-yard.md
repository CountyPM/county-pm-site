Not without written notice and one of the purposes the statute allows. Civil Code §1954 sets a closed list: an emergency, necessary or agreed repairs, decorations, alterations or improvements, agreed services, showing the unit to prospective purchasers, mortgagees, tenants, workers or contractors, a court order, the pre-move-out inspection under §1950.5(f), or where the tenant has abandoned or surrendered the premises. "Checking on the yard" is not on that list. Neither is driving by to see how things look.

Paying for the landscaping does not create an exception. Owners reason that because they hired and pay the gardener, the yard is still theirs to inspect — but the tenant's possessory interest runs to the whole premises they rented, yard included. Where the landscaping service is an agreed service under the lease, entry to supply it is permitted, which is a narrower thing than it sounds: it covers the service happening on a schedule the tenant knows about, not the owner walking the property whenever they like.

The notice itself has a shape. §1954 requires reasonable written notice of the intent to enter, stating the date, approximate time, and purpose, and 24 hours is presumed reasonable absent evidence to the contrary. Entry has to be during normal business hours unless the tenant consents at the time of entry to something else. And the statute says plainly that the right of access may not be abused or used to harass — repeated technically-noticed entries can themselves become the violation.

This one matters more than most in backyard tenancies. When the owner lives on the same parcel — in the main house with an ADU rented out, or the reverse — the distance between "I'm just out here anyway" and an unnoticed entry is a few steps, and the tenant experiences it as surveillance rather than maintenance. Set a standing, disclosed schedule for any landscaping service in the lease, give written notice for anything else, and keep a copy. The cost of getting this wrong is not theoretical: unnoticed entries on someone else's rented ground have produced substantial settlements.
