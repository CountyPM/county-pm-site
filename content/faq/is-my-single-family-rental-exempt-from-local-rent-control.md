Usually — but it turns on how you hold title, not on the fact that it's a single-family home. Under the state Costa-Hawkins Rental Housing Act, single-family homes and condominiums are generally exempt from local rent-control caps like Oxnard's or Ojai's, and unlike the statewide Tenant Protection Act, Costa-Hawkins doesn't require you to give the tenant any notice to claim it.

That exemption disappears the moment the ownership structure changes shape: it's lost if the property is owned by a corporation, by a real estate investment trust, or by an LLC with even one corporate member. It's also lost if there's a second unit or an ADU on the same lot — the exemption applies to the single-family use, not just the zoning. Family trusts are treated differently and generally do still qualify for the exemption, since the trust's settlor or beneficiary is treated as the individual owner.

Separately, the statewide Tenant Protection Act requires a specific exemption notice in the lease for single-family homes and condos to claim ITS exemption (Civil Code §1947.12) — a different requirement from Costa-Hawkins, and easy to miss if you're only thinking about the local ordinance. Owning through an LLC or a corporate structure for liability protection is common, and it's precisely the move that can cost you the local rent-cap exemption without changing anything about how the property is used.

This is general information, not legal advice; exemption status depends on your exact ownership structure and your specific city's ordinance, so confirm your situation with a qualified professional before relying on it.
