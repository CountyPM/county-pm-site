Yes. For an actual pet, a California landlord can charge monthly pet rent; no statewide statute sets or caps it. Pet rent is ongoing rent for the added wear an animal brings, not a deposit, so it should be written into the lease or a pet addendum with the amount, the animal it covers, and when it is due.

Pet rent is not part of the security deposit, and that distinction matters. California's deposit law, Civil Code §1950.5, generally limits everything you collect as security to one month's rent, and a "pet deposit" counts toward that same cap. Monthly pet rent sits outside the deposit: it is income you keep, not money you hold and return. That is why pet rent, rather than a bigger deposit, is now the main tool for pricing pet risk. Many owners set it per animal; $50 to $100 a month is a common range in our market.

Pet rent can never be charged for an assistance animal. Service animals and emotional support animals are not pets under the federal Fair Housing Act or California's Fair Employment and Housing Act, so pet rent, a pet deposit, or any other pet fee for one is a fair-housing violation. The tenant is still responsible for any actual damage the animal causes.

Starting April 1, 2027, your pet charges also need to be disclosed up front. SB 1296 (Civil Code §1942.7.5) requires every landlord to have a written pet policy or pet addendum, give a copy with each rental application, and link it in online listings where the platform allows. The policy must describe the rules and requirements for tenants with pets, so put your pet rent there. Check local ordinances as well: some cities with rent control regulate added charges.

This is general information, not legal advice; confirm your lease and pet addendum with a qualified professional.
