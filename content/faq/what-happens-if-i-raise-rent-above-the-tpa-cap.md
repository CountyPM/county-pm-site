At minimum, you owe the tenant the amount by which the rent you demanded, accepted, or retained exceeded the cap (5% plus the change in the cost of living, or 10%, whichever is lower, over the lowest rent charged in the prior 12 months), plus their attorney's fees and costs and a court order to stop. If a court finds you acted willfully, or with oppression, fraud, or malice, Civil Code §1947.12 allows damages up to three times the overcharge amount — not three times the rent, three times how much you overcharged.

Since SB 567 took effect in April 2024, a city attorney, county counsel, or the state Attorney General can pursue this independently of any one tenant filing suit, and recent enforcement actions have gone beyond writing a check: settlements have required restitution to affected tenants, monetary penalties, new rent-tracking policies, and multi-year compliance audits of the landlord's practices.

The exposure scales with pattern, not just amount. A single small overcharge caught early and corrected is a very different case from the same overcharge repeated across a dozen units for two years — the second is exactly the fact pattern that turns a demand letter into a city attorney's enforcement action.

This is general information, not legal advice; the damages and remedies that actually apply depend on your specific facts and whether willfulness can be shown, so confirm your situation with a qualified professional.
