Up to twice the amount of the deposit, on top of returning the deposit itself, if a court finds the landlord retained it in bad faith — that's the statutory penalty under Civil Code §1950.5, and it's assessed separately from and in addition to whatever actual damages the tenant can prove. Add in attorney's fees (which apply to both sides if the lease has an attorney-fee clause), court costs, and interest, and a dispute that started over a few hundred dollars in disputed cleaning charges can turn into a bill several times that size.

Bad faith is the operative word, and it's usually proven by absence of documentation, not by any single bad act: withholding deductions with no itemized statement, no receipts, and no move-in comparison is exactly the pattern small claims judges treat as bad faith rather than an honest disagreement about damage.

The defense is cheap and it's the same one that helps everywhere else in this hub: an itemized deduction statement sent within the statutory window, receipts or reasonable cost estimates for the work, and dated move-in and move-out photos to compare against. A landlord who can produce that file is arguing about whether specific charges were reasonable — a much better position than defending the decision to withhold money with nothing to show for it.

This is general information, not legal advice; whether a specific retention counts as bad faith turns on your documentation and the facts, so confirm your situation with a qualified professional.
