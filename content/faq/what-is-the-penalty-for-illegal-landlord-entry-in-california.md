There is no automatic fine for an ordinary bad entry. The $2,000 figure that circulates online comes from Civil Code §1940.2, and it reaches a narrower case than owners or tenants usually assume: the landlord must commit a **significant and intentional** violation of §1954 **and** do it for the purpose of influencing the tenant to vacate. Both conditions have to be met. A tenant who prevails in a civil action under §1940.2 may recover a civil penalty of up to $2,000 per violation.

An entry that is careless rather than coercive is handled differently. Ordinary over-entry — walking in without the written notice §1954 requires, entering outside normal business hours, entering for a purpose not on the statute's list — is addressed through actual damages on theories like breach of the covenant of quiet enjoyment, trespass, intrusion upon seclusion, and in severe cases constructive eviction. There is no per-violation number attached to those. What a tenant recovers depends on what the conduct actually did to them.

That distinction cuts both ways, and owners should not read it as reassurance. "No statutory penalty" is not the same as "no exposure." Damages for a pattern of intrusion on a tenant's home are fact-driven and can exceed $2,000 comfortably, they come with the possibility of attorney's fees depending on the theory pleaded, and §1954 itself prohibits abusing the right of access or using it to harass — so a series of technically-noticed entries can build the record that turns a nuisance into a claim.

The practical takeaway is that the defense is documentary and it is cheap. Written notice stating date, approximate time, and purpose; 24 hours ahead; normal business hours; a permitted purpose from the §1954 list; and a copy kept in the tenant file. An owner who can produce that sequence is not in the argument at all. An owner reconstructing it from memory a year later usually is.

This is general information, not legal advice; the exposure here turns on the specific conduct and the claims pleaded, so consult a qualified California attorney about your situation.
