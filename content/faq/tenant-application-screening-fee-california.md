California caps the tenant application screening fee at a statutory maximum that started at $30 in 1998 and adjusts upward each year for inflation — currently calculated at $65.86 for 2026 — and in no case may the fee exceed the landlord's actual out-of-pocket cost of screening plus the reasonable value of staff time spent obtaining the information, if that actual cost is lower than the cap. You must give the applicant a receipt itemizing the actual expenses and refund any part of the fee that wasn't used for screening.

Since AB 2493 took effect January 1, 2025, the fee itself is the smaller part of the obligation — the larger exposure is the process around it. You may not collect a screening fee at all if you know or should know that no unit is actually available or will be within a reasonable time. You must give every applicant your screening criteria in writing before you take a fee from them, and apply those criteria consistently. You must automatically send the applicant a copy of their own consumer credit report within seven days of receiving it — they don't have to ask. And you must commit to one of two processing paths and follow it: either process applications in the order completed applications arrive and offer the unit to the first applicant who meets your written criteria, or evaluate multiple applicants and refund the full screening fee to everyone you don't select, within seven days of choosing a tenant or thirty days of their application, whichever comes first. Mixing the two — quietly holding several applications open while you decide — is the pattern AB 2493 was written to stop.

Two related screening rules from the same legislative window affect what you can act on, separate from the fee itself. SB 267, effective January 1, 2024, bars using an applicant's credit history against them where a government rent subsidy is involved, unless you first offer them the chance to supply lawful, verifiable alternative evidence of their ability to pay their share of the rent, and give them a reasonable amount of time to produce it. SB 1061, effective January 1, 2025, keeps medical debt off California consumer credit reports in the first place, which changes what a credit report can even show you.

A bill that would have gone further — capping the fee at actual cost only, with no CPI-indexed ceiling above it — did not become that law. SB 381 (2025–26) originally proposed exactly that, but its tenant-screening provisions were stripped out during the 2026 legislative session and the bill was redirected to an unrelated subject; as of this writing it carries no screening-fee provision. The CPI-adjusted statutory cap remains the operative ceiling, and there's no pending replacement for it in the Legislature right now.

The practical shape of compliance in 2026: a written screening-criteria sheet handed out before any fee changes hands, a fee that tracks your real cost rather than functioning as a small profit center, an itemized receipt, automatic delivery of the credit report within seven days, and a processing rule you've actually chosen and can point to. Owners who treat the fee as the only compliance question are the ones most likely to get the process around it wrong.

This is general information, not legal advice; the current CPI-adjusted maximum changes annually and the process rules are detailed, so confirm your specific approach against Civil Code §1950.6 or with a qualified professional.
