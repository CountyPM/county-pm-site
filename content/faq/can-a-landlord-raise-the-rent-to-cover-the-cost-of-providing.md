Not on the basis of AB 628 alone. The refrigerator-and-stove rule amends Civil Code §1941.1 to make those appliances part of habitability — it is an obligation you owe, not a reason you may raise rent. Any increase still has to clear every rule that already governs the property: the AB 1482 cap where it applies, Costa-Hawkins, and any local ordinance. You also cannot offer a rent discount in place of actually providing the required refrigerator.

There is no surcharge line in a California residential lease. Whatever the new cost is — an appliance under AB 628, a 35% insurance renewal, a water bill — it can only be absorbed into rent, and rent moves only within whatever cap covers the unit. That is why "can I pass this through?" is always the same question wearing different clothes, and the answer always turns on the cap rather than on the expense.

Where AB 1482 covers the unit, the room you have is smaller than the headline percentage suggests. The cap is the lower of 5% plus the applicable CPI or 10% in any 12-month period, but Civil Code §1947.12(a)(1) measures it against the **lowest** gross rental rate charged at any time in the preceding 12 months, and §1947.12(a)(2) allows no more than two increments in that window. There is no banking. An increase you were entitled to take in an earlier year and skipped is not stored for later, and because every subsequent year computes off a base that never moved, skipping compounds. An owner who held rent flat through 2025 does not get to catch up now — the refrigerator is priced into this year's allowance or it is not priced in at all.

Two arithmetic traps sit in front of the notice itself. The CPI component depends on which metropolitan-area index covers your property, and published guidance for Ventura County is not consistent on that point, so pull the current figure for your specific area before you calculate — a wrong number makes the notice defective no matter how reasonable the increase. Separately, Civil Code §827 governs how much warning you give, independent of the cap: at least 30 days where the increase is 10% or less of the rent charged at any time in the prior 12 months, at least 90 days where it is more than 10%, counting cumulative increases in that period. An increase can sit comfortably inside the cap and still be void because it was served on 30 days' notice when it needed 90.

Where you own changes the ceiling. Ojai's Ordinance No. 937, effective April 28, 2023, caps covered units at 4% with one increase per 12-month period — tighter than the state formula on both dimensions — and offers a petition to the Community Development Director for a fair-return increase above the cap, a process that involves tenant notification and a response period. Oxnard's Ordinance 3013 runs a comparable 4%, one-increase-per-year cap, though its rent cap reaches multifamily property first occupied before February 1, 1995, and most single-family homes and condominiums fall outside it under Costa-Hawkins. If your property sits in either city, work from the ordinance and treat the state number as irrelevant to you.

In the rest of Ventura County, many single-family rentals are exempt from the AB 1482 cap — but only where the owner is a natural person rather than a REIT, corporation, or corporate-member LLC, and only where the prescribed exemption notice is actually in the lease. Exemption from the cap is not exemption from §827 notice periods, and it is not exemption from the habitability duty AB 628 created. Pull the executed lease before you assume which set of rules you are operating under.

This is general information, not legal advice; rent caps, local ordinances, and CPI figures change annually, so confirm your situation with a qualified professional before you serve a notice.
