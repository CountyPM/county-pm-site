On October 16 I published The Private Property Rights Voter Guide with 19 firm recommendations for anyone who owns a home, a rental, or any property in Ventura County. The votes are counted. Voters agreed with five of them.

I'm not going to soften that. If you're going to tell people how to vote, you owe them the results, especially the ones that went the other way. Here they are, followed by the questions I think they raise.

## Where owners won

- **Proposition 40, the billionaire tax — rejected.** Voters said no to a one-time 5% tax on assets, 52% to 48%. The first wealth tax on a California ballot failed.
- **Proposition 42, no new taxes on personal property — passed.** It bans new state taxes on retirement accounts, stocks, and business interests. The margin was thin, about 51%, but a firewall at 51% is still a firewall.
- **Measure D, the United Water Conservation District bond — passed.** It needed two-thirds and got nearly 70%. Remember that number.
- **Sarah Rascón, State Senate District 26 — lost.** She campaigned on repealing Costa-Hawkins and the Ellis Act and reforming Proposition 13. Sara Hernandez beat her 53% to 47%.
- **Sam Sukaton, Board of Equalization District 3 — lost.** He ran on "fixing" Prop. 13. Mike Gipson won by nearly 20 points.

## Where owners lost

- **Proposition 43 — rejected.** The most important measure on the ballot. It would have restored the two-thirds vote for local special taxes placed on the ballot by initiative, closing the loophole behind Los Angeles's "mansion tax." The loophole stays open.
- **Proposition 3 — passed.** The "temporary" high-income tax rates from 2012 are now permanent. Sell a rental with a large gain and that year can land you in those brackets, with no sunset to wait for.
- **Proposition 45 — rejected.** The fast-track for housing under the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA) failed.
- **Propositions 1 and 2 — passed.** The $11.25 billion housing bond at 61%, the rainy-day fund changes at 60%.
- **Every local bond the guide opposed — passed.** Rio School District's Measure B (68%), Santa Paula Unified's Measure C (59%), and Allan Hancock College's Measure H (56%). Simi Valley's hotel tax, Measure A, passed with 71%.
- **Justices Armen Tamzarian and Audra Mori — retained**, at roughly 70% yes each. They wrote and joined the opinion upholding the mansion tax. The ruling that keeps the Prop. 43 loophole alive cost them nothing at the ballot box.
- **Sally Lieber, Board of Equalization District 2 — won**, 60% to 40%.

On the "your call" items: Oxnard's sales-tax renewal (Measure E) failed narrowly, and Las Virgenes's parcel-tax renewal (Measure LV) passed with about 71%.

## The Democratic Socialists of America had a good night, with one exception

Nithya Raman, endorsed by the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA), is now mayor of Los Angeles. Jane Kim is Insurance Commissioner. Randy Villegas won the 22nd Congressional District. DSA opposed Prop. 43 and won; DSA backed Prop. 40 and lost.

The exception: the two candidates whose platforms named rent-control expansion and Prop. 13 reform outright both lost, and not narrowly. Voters will elect a DSA-endorsed mayor. They are not yet sending Sacramento a senator who campaigns on repealing Costa-Hawkins.

## What Measure D proves

Opponents of Prop. 43 said a two-thirds threshold makes it impossible to fund anything. Ventura County voters answered in the same election: Measure D asked for $350 million, needed two-thirds, and got 70%. When voters believe in what they're paying for, a supermajority isn't a wall. It's a filter. The mansion tax never had to pass through it.

## The questions this leaves

Five of nineteen is not a rounding error, and I don't think it's a messaging problem. It's worth sitting with what it might mean.

- If voters will protect their retirement accounts (Prop. 42) but not their property-tax structure (Prop. 43), what are they telling us about which assets they expect to own?
- Every bond and every tax on real property passed. Every candidate who threatened the rental stock lost. Is the electorate protecting housing it *lives in* rather than housing it *owns*?
- The median first-time homebuyer in America is now 40 years old, and first-time buyers are half the share they were before 2008. How many of the people who voted on November 3 expect to pay a property-tax bill in the next decade?
- If the coalition that protected Prop. 13 for 48 years is shrinking by demographics alone, the fix isn't a better argument. It's more owners.
- So who is going to speak for ownership before the next ballot is written?
