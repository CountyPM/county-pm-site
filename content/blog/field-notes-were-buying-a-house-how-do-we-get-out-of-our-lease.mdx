The call came in Monday. Good tenants, paid on time, about to make an offer on a house. The lease runs to the end of next July. "What are our options?"

There are three. Two of them are honorable.

## Door one: we re-rent it, and the clock runs on you

This is the default in California. A residential landlord cannot sit on an empty house and bill you through July. Civil Code section 1951.2 requires the owner to mitigate — to make reasonable efforts to find a replacement tenant. (The sit-and-bill posture exists in commercial leasing under section 1951.4, which is why people have heard of it. It does not apply to your house.)

So my job becomes finding a replacement at the same rent and terms. Not higher — if the owner marks the rent up while holding you to the balance, the mitigation argument collapses. The owner can go lower to get it rented, and if so you owe the difference for the rest of the term. The day the new tenant moves in, your liability ends. What you owe is the rent for the days in between, any shortfall, and the lease fee the owner paid to re-rent the place, which in our office is half a month's rent.

The catch is the word *between*. In March, that gap might be two weeks. This is December. You don't know how long it will take, and neither do I.

## Door two: buy your way out at a fixed price

The alternative is a negotiated cancellation. The owner's instinct is almost always the same: thirty days' notice plus one month's rent. What the owner is really asking me is, "Can you fill it inside sixty days?" If I can, the owner comes out ahead. If I can't, the tenant does.

From the tenant's side, the fee sounds like a penalty. It isn't. It is door one's rent differential, prepaid and capped. You have converted an open-ended liability into a fixed cost, and in a winter market that is usually the cheaper exit.

## Door three: keys under the mat

Some tenants move out, slide the keys under the door, and head for the new house. This cancels nothing. The lease is still in force, the mitigation clock still runs, and the balance still follows you — to collections, and to the credit report the lender on your new house is about to pull. Don't.

## What the market was already saying

Before I called the owner, I ran the numbers. The home's floor plan had leased six times in the same community over the last two years, between $3,500 and $3,950, and most of those listings had been cut from a higher price before they moved. Three homes in the community were on the market that day at $3,900 to $3,950. One of them — a larger floor plan two streets over — had been sitting thirty-nine days with no application. The sister home with the identical plan had taken seventy-six days to lease last December, at $3,500.

A bigger house failing at the price you'd like to ask is the market's answer, delivered in advance.

## What I told the owner

The owner wanted the fixed fee. Fine — but I did not want to park that money in the operating account and then list the house at full price into the worst leasing month of the year, next to three competitors.

Instead: price the house a notch *under* the competition, and let the cancellation fee fund the difference. List at $3,650 instead of $3,900. The gap over a twelve-month lease is $3,000. A one-month fee of $3,900 covers it with $900 left over.

Here is the part that surprises owners. A full year at $3,650 plus the $3,900 fee is $47,700. A full year at $3,900 with *zero* vacancy is $46,800. The buydown beats the best possible outcome of holding the price — before you count a single empty day. And a year at $3,900 with zero vacancy is not what this market was offering.

If you've bought a house, you've seen this before. It's a rate buydown. Pay points up front to lower the monthly cost and make the deal move. The cancellation fee is the points.

## What the paper says

The whole arrangement fits on one page, an early-termination addendum: the termination date, which doubles as the tenant's thirty-day notice; the one-month fee to the owner, which buys the release; the half-month re-leasing fee to the manager; the release itself, effective at the end of the notice period once both fees are paid; and the notice of the tenant's right to a pre-move-out inspection under Civil Code section 1950.5. Nothing is a handshake.

One note for the tenant: give notice now, not after the holidays. Those thirty days are also the manager's pre-marketing window. If the house is shown while you're still in it, the gap between your last day and the next tenant's first day can be close to zero — and that is exactly what makes the fixed fee a good deal for you.

## Everyone wins

For a lot of owners, a vacancy is simply lost income — there is no fee to soften it, and the month is gone. This case was different, because there was money on the table that doesn't normally exist, and the question was only what to do with it. Used as a penalty, it sits in an account while the house sits empty. Used as a buydown, it fills the house. The owner nets more than a full-price year. The departing tenants get a clean release and a known cost. The new tenant gets the best-priced home in the neighborhood. And the house doesn't spend December dark.

Every lease break comes down to one thing: who holds the vacancy risk. Door one leaves it on the tenant. Door two moves it to the owner for a price. Door three pretends it doesn't exist. The buydown is the version where nobody holds it very long, because nobody has to.
