About nine months after I placed a tenant in one of our homes, the owner called me. His tenant had asked to start paying rent in the middle of the month instead of on the first. He wanted to know what to do.

I gave him the legal answer first. In California, the first step when rent isn't paid is a 3-day notice to pay rent or quit, and if that doesn't work, an eviction. I've handled a lot of nonpayment cases in my career and never lost one. But I didn't recommend starting there.

I asked him to do something first: talk to her. Why was paying on the first suddenly hard?

## What did the conversation turn up?

Her hours at work had been cut. When the owner called her back, he learned she was a single mother with two kids still at home, one in high school. Going forward, the rent was going to strain the family budget every month. A mid-month due date wouldn't fix that. It would just move the problem two weeks down the calendar.

That changed the question. It was no longer how to collect late rent. It was how to end the lease early without hurting her or the owner.

## What deal did the owner and tenant work out?

They agreed to a short, clean exit:

- **She paid that month's rent** and agreed to move out the following month.
- **She cooperated with showings** while she was still living there, so we could find the next tenant quickly.
- **Her security deposit covered her final month's rent.** The owner had recently been in the home and knew there was no damage, so there was nothing else for the deposit to cover.
- **The owner released her from any further liability** under the lease.

With her cooperation, I found a new tenant. The owner had very little vacancy. She left with her rental history clean, no judgment against her, and no bill hanging over her for the rest of the lease. Her kids didn't go through an eviction.

## Why did this beat the 3-day notice?

The 3-day notice is the right tool when a tenant won't pay. It's the wrong tool when a tenant can't keep paying and is willing to work with you.

Look at what eviction would have cost this owner: weeks of lost rent, attorney fees, a court date, and in Ventura County today, a timeline that can stretch for months. At the end of it, he'd have a judgment against a tenant with no money to pay it, and a home he couldn't show while she was still in it.

California law also limits what an owner can collect from a tenant who leaves early. The owner has a duty to re-rent the home, and the tenant generally owes only the rent lost until a new tenant starts paying (California Civil Code §1951.2). In a market like ours, where good homes don't sit empty long, that window is usually short. So a negotiated exit often gets the owner close to what he'd recover anyway, without the cost or the wait.

## What do I tell owners now?

Pick up the phone before you serve anything. When a tenant starts struggling with rent, ask what changed. Sometimes it's a one-time problem and a short payment plan is enough. Sometimes, like here, the honest answer is that the home doesn't fit the family's budget anymore, and the kindest and most profitable thing is to help them leave well.

When we do reach that point, we put it in writing. Today County Property Management (CPM) uses a short Early Termination Agreement that sets the move-out date, what the tenant pays, how the deposit is handled, showing cooperation, and the release. It's voluntary. If the tenant doesn't sign, the lease stays in effect. Most tenants sign, because they'd rather know the number now than face an open-ended bill later.

The 3-day notice will still be there if the conversation fails. In my experience it rarely needs to be the first move.
