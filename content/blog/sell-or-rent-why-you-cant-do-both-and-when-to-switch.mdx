Owners ask me some version of this all the time: "My house is listed for sale. Can I also try to rent it, just in case?"

It's a reasonable question. In practice, doing both at once usually weakens both. Zillow won't even display a property as for sale and for rent at the same time. A "For Sale" sign tells rental applicants they may have to move in six months, so the strongest tenants, the ones who want a full 12-month lease, keep looking. Meanwhile, buyers who hear "might be tenant-occupied" picture scheduling headaches.

So the real question isn't "Can I do both?" It's "What's my primary objective, and at what point do I switch?"

## Decide the Objective First

If you listed to sell, selling is the objective. Trust your agent, price it right, and give the market a fair chance. Leasing isn't a parallel track. It's Plan B, and it should stay on the shelf until a clear trigger tells you to use it.

The mistake I see most often is having no trigger at all. The owner keeps carrying an empty house, month after month, hoping next weekend's open house is the one. By the time they decide to rent, they've spent money they didn't need to and are making the call under pressure.

## Set Your Trigger Before You Need It

Before the house goes on the market, or right now if it's already listed, write down two numbers:

1. **A date.** How long will you give the sale? Base it on what your agent tells you about typical days on market for your price range, plus a reasonable cushion.
2. **A carrying-cost limit.** Add up what the empty house costs you each month: mortgage, property taxes, insurance, utilities, HOA (homeowners association) dues, and yard and pool upkeep. Then decide how many months of that you can absorb without real strain.

Whichever comes first is when Plan B starts. Making the decision in advance, while you're calm, beats making it in month five when you're frustrated and writing checks.

Share the trigger with your listing agent. A good agent will respect it, and it keeps everyone honest about pricing along the way.

## Signals That You're Getting Close

Even before you hit your date or dollar limit, watch for these:

- **Price reductions aren't producing showings.** If two reductions haven't changed the traffic, the market is telling you something.
- **Feedback has gone quiet.** No second showings, no offers, no questions from buyers' agents.
- **The season is turning.** Rental demand in Ventura County is strongest in spring and summer, when families move between school years. If your sale drags into fall, waiting until next spring to rent can mean a long, expensive vacancy.

## Plan B Is a Pivot, Not a Pause

When you pull the trigger, commit to it:

- **Withdraw the sale listing completely** and take down the sign. Check what your listing agreement says about withdrawing or canceling before you do.
- **Re-market for renters.** That means new photos, a rental description, and pricing based on rental comparables rather than sale comps.
- **Lease for a full term.** Twelve months attracts the best applicants and gives you stable income.

Understand what leasing changes. Once a tenant moves in, you're selling to investors rather than owner-occupants, and tenants have rights to proper notice before showings. If you plan to sell again, the cleanest time is usually when the lease ends.

## The Bottom Line

Selling and renting at the same time rarely works. Pick your objective, trust your agent with it, and decide in advance exactly when you'll switch. Then, if the sale doesn't come together, you aren't scrambling. You're just executing the plan.
