## What is changing on single-family lots?

A single-family lot in California can now legally hold far more than one home. State law requires cities to approve accessory dwelling units (ADUs) by checklist, with no public hearing and no neighbor veto. Assembly Bill (AB) 956, signed by Governor Newsom on September 29, 2026 and effective January 1, 2027, doubles the number of new detached ADUs a city must approve on a single-family lot, from one to two. On a large lot, that means a main house and two backyard homes, and in some cases another unit carved from the house or garage.

Your city's zoning map won't show any of this. It still says "single-family." But the map now describes what a street looked like, not what it can legally become.

## Did my city actually rezone my neighborhood?

No. Your city's zoning designation hasn't changed. What changed is that state law now overrides it for ADUs. Cities must approve qualifying units ministerially, meaning by a checklist of objective standards rather than a discretionary vote. The practical effect looks like a rezoning; the legal mechanism is state preemption.

Local codes also tend to lag. It often takes months for cities to rewrite their ordinances after a state law changes, and until they do, the state law controls where the two conflict.

## Why are older neighborhoods changing first?

Change shows up first where the space already exists. Postwar Ventura County tracts have what newer subdivisions don't: deep lots, detached garages, alley access, and often no homeowners association (HOA). Newer planned communities squeezed their lots. The 1950s and 60s didn't. And where an HOA does exist, AB 956 makes clear it can't block an ADU that complies with state law.

That's why the first multi-unit "houses" are appearing on quiet, established streets, not in new developments.

## Is it really the same as a fourplex?

No, and owners should understand the differences before they build:

- **You generally can't sell the units separately** unless your city has adopted the state's optional condominium-style ADU sales rules.
- **Each unit has size, height, and setback limits.**
- **Unit types involve trade-offs.** A city doesn't have to approve a junior accessory dwelling unit (JADU) on a lot that already has two new detached ADUs, and a JADU requires the owner to live on the property. We compare the options in [Interior Junior ADU, Garage Conversion, or Detached ADU: What Each One Really Costs](https://www.c-p-m.com/blog/interior-junior-adu-garage-conversion-or-detached-adu-what-each-one-really-costs).
- **Septic systems, sewer capacity, and fire hazard zones** rule out more lots than zoning ever did.
- **Adding units can change your rent cap status** under AB 1482, California's Tenant Protection Act. See [Building an ADU Can Cost You Your Exemption Before You Ever Rent It](https://www.c-p-m.com/blog/building-an-adu-can-cost-you-your-exemption-before-you-ever-rent-it).

There's also one large advantage over buying an actual fourplex. Under Proposition 13, only the new construction is assessed. The main house keeps its existing tax base. Buying a fourplex resets the tax base on the entire property.

## Why doesn't the value show up yet?

Appraisers have three ways to value property:

1. **Sales comparison:** what similar properties nearby sold for
2. **Cost:** what it would cost to build, less depreciation
3. **Income:** what the property earns

For houses, appraisers rely almost entirely on sales comparison, and lenders rely on the appraiser. Few multi-unit "houses" on single-family streets have sold yet, so comparable sales are scarce. The appraiser compares the property to the plain houses next door, and the loan reflects that lower figure. Loan programs built around a house with one ADU don't fit neatly either, so the buyer pool narrows to people with cash, larger down payments, or lenders willing to underwrite the rents.

An investor asks a different question: what does the property earn? Measured that way, the cash flow from additional units can move the value well beyond what a house-to-house comparison shows.

## What happens when the market catches up?

Appraisals and lending follow sales. As more of these properties sell to buyers who price on income, comparable sales will appear, appraisals will follow them, and financing will adjust. Owners who understand this early are in a strong position. Owners who sell a large lot priced as "just a house" may leave money on the table.

If you do sell after building, know the tax picture first: a rented detached ADU may not share your home sale exclusion. We explain why in [Your Detached ADU Was Never Covered by Your Home Sale Exclusion](https://www.c-p-m.com/blog/your-detached-adu-was-never-covered-by-your-home-sale-exclusion).

## What should neighbors expect?

More people on the same street, more cars, and backyard cottages where there used to be lawn. Some neighbors will welcome it as housing for their grown children and aging parents; others will feel the street changing around them. Either way, the change is being decided in Sacramento, not at city hall. The useful response is to understand it before it arrives next door.

## What should owners of large lots do now?

Don't price, sell, or plan around the zoning map alone. Before you list a large-lot property or decide what to do with it, find out what state law now allows on that specific parcel: setbacks, utilities, septic or sewer, fire zone, and any local rules your city still enforces. Then look at the property the way an income buyer would, not just the way an appraiser will.
