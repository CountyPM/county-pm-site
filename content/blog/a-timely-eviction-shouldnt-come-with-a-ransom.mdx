When a tenant stops paying rent in California, the law promises a fast process. An eviction case, formally called an unlawful detainer, is meant to be a summary proceeding, and the statute calls for trial within 20 days of a request to set one. In practice, a contested case can run for months. That gap between what the law promises and what courts deliver is the real problem, and owners end up paying to make it go away.

## How a Stalled Eviction Case Unfolds

It starts with a 3-day notice to pay rent or quit, with weekends and court holidays excluded. If the rent isn't paid, the owner files suit, and the tenant has 10 court days to respond. Then the delays stack up:

- **Fee waiver.** A tenant who qualifies financially can file a defense at no cost, including the jury fee deposit.
- **Jury demand.** A jury trial takes far longer to schedule and multiplies the owner's attorney costs. Some self-help answer forms make demanding a jury as easy as checking a box.
- **Free counsel.** In jurisdictions that fund tenant attorneys, even a weak defense can run its full course.
- **Continuances and congested calendars.** These stretch the remaining weeks into months.

Each step is legal. Together they mean the tenant lives rent-free during the case while the owner pays the mortgage, the property taxes, the insurance, and the lawyer.

## What the Delay Really Costs

On a home renting for $3,000 a month, four months of delay means $12,000 in lost rent before attorney fees and turnover costs. The court will usually award a judgment for the unpaid rent, but a judgment against someone who couldn't pay rent is rarely collected.

## Cash-for-Keys Is a Ransom, Not a Solution

That math is why so many owners end up paying tenants to leave. A $3,000 payment to avoid a $15,000 loss is a rational choice. But consider what it means: an owner who has done everything right pays the person who stopped paying, because the court can't decide a simple case on time.

A justice system shouldn't need private payments to function. Every cash-for-keys deal is evidence the process is broken.

## What Should Change

- **Enforce the deadline that already exists.** Trial within 20 days should mean 20 days, jury or not. If courts lack the judges or staff to meet it, fund dedicated eviction calendars.
- **Require rent into court during any delay the tenant requests.** If a tenant asks for a jury or a continuance, the accruing rent should be deposited with the court. California law already allows a narrow version of this. It should be routine. Tenants with real defenses lose nothing, and stalling stops being free.
- **Limit continuances.** Grant them for genuine cause, not by default.
- **Make jury demands informed.** Tenants should choose a jury deliberately, with a plain explanation of what it means, not through a pre-checked box.
- **Balance access to help.** Where tenants receive publicly funded counsel, small owners with one or two units deserve access to fast-track mediation or legal clinics.

None of this takes away a tenant's right to a defense. It simply makes delay cost the person causing it.

## What Owners Can Do Until Then

Reform takes time and organized pressure, the kind local rental housing associations exist to apply. Meanwhile, I've never lost a nonpayment eviction, and that's mostly because of three habits:

### 1. Screen as if the eviction already happened

Verify income, call prior landlords rather than only the current one, and apply the same written criteria to every applicant.

### 2. Serve notices that can't be attacked

Get the amount, the deadline, the payment instructions, and the method of service exactly right. A defective notice can get a case dismissed and reset the clock.

### 3. Act on day three, not day twenty

A short payment plan or a referral to rental assistance solves many problems while they're still small.

## The Bottom Line

The law already promises a fast eviction process. Courts should deliver it. Until they do, owners will keep paying a ransom the system never should have charged.
