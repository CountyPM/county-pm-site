The Ground Beneath Your Feet — Private Property and the Free Society

## The Ground Beneath Your Feet

A job puts food on the table. Real estate builds the table itself. Private property isn't a luxury of the wealthy — it's the last honest ladder left in America.

Every generation has to relearn the same lesson. Wealth is not a salary. A paycheck — even a good one — is permission to survive. It covers the rent, the groceries, the car payment. It keeps the lights on. With discipline and a little luck, it might even support a family. That is no small thing. Work is dignity. A man who earns his way earns his self-respect, and nothing in a free society is more foundational than that. But a job, by itself, does not build wealth. It builds a life. Real estate builds a future.

The distinction matters, and we have spent forty years blurring it. We told people that if they worked hard enough, long enough, and trusted the system, the system would reward them. Sometimes it did. More often, it handed them a pension they couldn't count on, a 401(k) hostage to markets they don't control, and a retirement that looked nothing like the one they were promised. The mechanism for genuine, durable, multigenerational wealth — private property — was priced out of reach, regulated into complexity, or simply never explained.

## What Property Actually Does

Real estate does something no other asset class does with the same reliability for the average person: it lets you borrow against someone else's money to build equity of your own. A working family that puts ten percent down on a modest home has acquired a leveraged asset that historically appreciates, provides shelter that would otherwise cost rent, generates tax advantages unavailable to renters, and can be refinanced, borrowed against, or eventually sold to fund the next chapter. None of that requires a finance degree. None of it requires inherited capital. It requires only the willingness to get in — and stay in.

That last part is what gets missed. The wealth is not in the purchase. The wealth is in the holding. People who bought modest homes in Ventura County thirty years ago and simply kept them are sitting on retirement accounts they never knew they were building. The ones who sold at the first sign of trouble — who treated real estate like a stock and chased the next thing — largely aren't. Patience in real estate is not a virtue. It is the strategy.

A job gives you the means to enter the game. Property is what keeps you in it — across recessions, elections, and everything in between.

## The Political Architecture of Ownership

The Founders were not romantics about human nature. They were students of history, and history had shown them what happened to people who owned nothing: they depended entirely on those who did. The right to private property was not inserted into the framework of American liberty as an afterthought or a concession to landowners. It was understood as a precondition for every other right. A man with no property has no independence. A man with no independence cannot exercise liberty in any meaningful sense. He can vote, in theory. But he votes from a position of dependence, and dependence is a powerful editor of conscience.

This is not a conservative argument or a liberal one. It is a structural one. Every political philosophy that has attempted to sever the link between the individual and private property has produced the same result: concentrated power, diminished freedom, and eventually, collapse. Not because capitalism is morally superior in some abstract sense, but because ownership distributes power. Distributed power is harder to abuse. It is the closest thing to a failsafe that a free society has.

## Hope Is Not a Sentiment. It Is an Asset.

Here is the argument that doesn't get made often enough, because it sounds dramatic until you look at the evidence: societies where ordinary people cannot accumulate property are not merely economically disadvantaged. They are unstable. The historical record on this is consistent enough to constitute a pattern. When a working population has no realistic path to ownership — when the dream of a better material life is not just difficult but structurally impossible — that population does not accept its circumstances quietly. It does not conclude that the system is fair and that they simply didn't try hard enough. It concludes, correctly, that the game is rigged. And rigged games eventually get flipped.

We are not talking about revolution as a theoretical concern. We are talking about what happens to civic life when hope is systematically extinguished. Crime rises. Institutions are delegitimized. Political extremes fill the vacuum left by collapsed faith in middle-class possibility. The most effective anti-poverty program, the most effective anti-radicalization program, the most effective civic stabilizer ever devised is the same thing: give ordinary people a stake in the system. A man with a mortgage, a rental property, a piece of land — that man has something to protect. He is not a revolutionary. He is an owner, which means he has skin in the game of social order.

## The Ladder Is Still There

The ladder hasn't disappeared. But it has been made harder to find, and in some markets, harder to reach. Zoning restrictions, rent control ordinances, and well-intentioned regulations that reduce housing supply have collectively made entry-level ownership more difficult in exactly the communities where it matters most. Policy that prices young families out of ownership doesn't protect renters. It sentences them to permanent tenancy, which is to say, permanent dependence on someone else's asset.

The answer is not to tear down the system of private property. The answer is to defend it — and to expand access to it. First-time buyer programs, down payment assistance, financial literacy that begins in high school rather than escrow — these are not socialist interventions. They are investments in the ownership class that a free society depends on to function.

Get a good job. Work hard. Earn your life. But do not stop there. The moment you can get into real estate — a first home, a duplex, a small rental — get in and hold on. That asset will compound in ways your salary cannot. It will outlast your working years, survive market corrections, and give the next generation a head start you may not have had yourself. That is how ordinary people build extraordinary stability. It always has been.

Private property is not a political slogan. It is the mechanism by which a free people keep their freedom — quietly, persistently, one deed at a time.
