Most Ventura County property managers advertise a monthly fee somewhere between 6% and 10% of rent. Owners compare those percentages and pick the lowest one. That is usually the wrong way to shop. The monthly percentage tells you very little about what you will actually pay over a year, and nothing about what happens when a tenancy goes wrong.

I have managed rentals in this county since 1986 and worked in California landlord-tenant matters since 1978. Here is how I would evaluate a property manager if I were the owner.

## Why is the monthly percentage misleading?

The percentage covers only part of the work. Many companies advertise a low monthly rate and then charge separately for everything around it. The common add-ons:

- **Leasing fees.** Often 50% to 100% of one month's rent each time the home is re-rented.
- **Renewal fees.** A flat charge when an existing tenant signs a new lease.
- **Maintenance markups.** A percentage added on top of every vendor invoice.
- **Inspection fees.** Charged per walk-through.
- **Court and eviction fees.** Hourly charges if a case ends up in front of a judge.
- **Vacancy or setup fees.** Charged when the property is empty or first comes under management.

None of these is wrong in itself. The problem is comparing an all-inclusive rate against a low headline number that has five line items hiding behind it.

## What does that look like on a real house?

Take a single-family home renting for $3,000 a month. The following is an illustration with assumptions stated, not any specific company's fees.

**Manager A: 8%, all-inclusive.** $240 a month, $2,880 a year. Nothing else.

**Manager B: 6% plus typical add-ons.**

- $180 a month in management fees, or $2,160 a year.
- A 75% leasing fee ($2,250) on a turnover every two years averages $1,125 a year.
- A $250 renewal fee in the off years averages $125 a year.
- A 10% markup on $4,000 of annual repairs adds $400.
- The total is about **$3,810 a year**, before any inspection or court charges.

The "cheaper" manager costs roughly $900 more a year in this example. Your numbers will differ. The point is to ask for the full fee schedule and run your own property through it.

## What should the fee actually buy you?

The work that protects an owner's money is mostly invisible until something goes wrong.

### Documentation

California's security deposit law (Civil Code §1950.5) was tightened again for 2026, and deposit disputes are won or lost on paperwork done at move-in, during the tenancy, and at move-out. I've appeared in small claims court on deposit disputes many times, and the one deposit case I remember losing turned on a single missed notice. I wrote about it in [The Night Before](https://www.c-p-m.com/blog/the-night-before), and about what a file that holds up looks like in [The File That Holds](https://www.c-p-m.com/blog/the-file-that-holds). Ask any manager you interview how they document a tenancy, and ask to see a sample move-in report.

### Legal compliance

Most Ventura County rentals fall under the statewide rent cap and just-cause eviction rules of Assembly Bill 1482 (AB 1482). On top of that, some cities, Oxnard most notably, layer on their own rules, and the unincorporated county has its own. A property a few miles away can sit under different rules from yours. Your manager should know which rules apply to your address without looking them up.

### Handling it when a tenancy fails

Evictions in Ventura County that once took about a month now commonly take four months or longer. A manager who prepares every tenancy as if it might end in court shortens that timeline and improves the outcome. I have never lost a nonpayment eviction, and that record comes from preparation long before the notice is served.

## How do you compare property managers?

Ask every company you interview the same questions:

1. **What is every fee I could pay in a year?** Get the written schedule, not the sales summary.
2. **Who will actually handle my property, and how many doors do they manage?**
3. **How do you document move-in condition, and what does the tenant receive?**
4. **What happens if my tenant stops paying?** Walk through the timeline and who pays for what.
5. **How long have you managed in this county, and in my city specifically?**
6. **Who holds the broker's license, and is that person involved in day-to-day decisions?** Check the license on the California Department of Real Estate (DRE) website.

## The bottom line

The cheapest property manager is the one with the lowest total cost over the life of your tenancy, including the year it goes badly. Ask for the full fee schedule, run your own property through it, and choose the manager whose documentation you'd want in front of a judge.
