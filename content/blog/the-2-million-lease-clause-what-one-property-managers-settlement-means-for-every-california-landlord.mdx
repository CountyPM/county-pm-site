## A settlement over eleven months

On November 19, 2026, California Attorney General Rob Bonta announced a $2 million settlement with Wright Equities, Inc., a property management company operating in the Central Valley. Of that, $1.73 million goes to people who were turned away from housing, and the rest covers the state's costs. The company also agreed to rewrite its rental policies, retrain its staff, and submit to at least four years of independent monitoring.

The headline violation was a lease term. Wright Equities required an 11-month initial lease at a number of its properties. That's it. No refusal to rent, no "we don't take Section 8" on the phone, no slur in an email. Just a number in a policy.

## Why eleven months was the whole case

Section 8, formally the Housing Choice Voucher program, is governed by federal rules. One of them (24 CFR 982.309) requires the initial lease term to be at least 12 months unless the local housing authority approves something shorter. So a policy that says "initial term is 11 months, no exceptions" doesn't inconvenience voucher holders. It excludes them. Every single one.

California closed this door in 2020 when Senate Bill 329 amended the Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) to make "source of income" a protected class, with housing subsidies like vouchers explicitly included. That means two things for an owner. First, you can't refuse an applicant because they pay with a voucher. Second, and this is the part that caught Wright Equities, you can't maintain a neutral-sounding rule that has a discriminatory effect on voucher holders, even if you never think about who it screens out.

The California Department of Justice found both. Investigators concluded the 11-month term was chosen *because* it disqualified voucher holders, which is intentional discrimination. But they also found the policy had an unlawful disparate impact by race, sex, familial status, and age, because voucher holders are disproportionately members of those groups. That second finding is the one every landlord should sit with. Intent is hard to prove. Impact is a spreadsheet.

## The two findings most owners will skip past

The lease term got the press release, but the settlement names two other practices, and they look a lot more like everyday screening habits than a scheme.

**Steering.** Wright Equities pointed voucher holders toward certain complexes and, in effect, away from others. If you manage more than one property and you find yourself saying "that one's probably not a fit, but let me show you this one" to voucher applicants and not to anyone else, that's steering.

**Misstating the criteria.** The company concealed or misrepresented its income and credit requirements, leaving voucher holders believing they wouldn't qualify or discouraging them from applying at all. Under California law (Government Code §12955(o)(1)(A)), once a government rent subsidy is in the picture, any income standard must be applied to the tenant's share of rent, not the full contract rent. An owner who quotes "three times the rent" without adjusting for the subsidy has just told a qualified applicant not to bother. And since January 1, 2024, §12955(o)(1)(B) adds a second rule: if you screen on credit history, a voucher applicant must be allowed to offer other evidence of their ability to pay their share, such as benefit records, pay stubs, or bank statements, and you have to give them reasonable time to produce it.

Neither of these requires bad intent. Both are the kind of thing a leasing agent does on autopilot.

## What a defensible screening policy looks like

At County Property Management, this is how we keep a lease term from becoming a lawsuit. None of it is complicated.

1. **Lease terms are preferences, not walls.** We can prefer a 12-month term, or avoid December move-outs, for ordinary business reasons. But the written criteria say terms are adjusted to meet housing authority requirements, and we mean it.
2. **Income is tested on the tenant's portion.** If the voucher covers $1,800 of a $2,400 rent, the applicant needs to qualify on $600. The criteria say so in writing.
3. **Credit screening has an alternative path.** A voucher applicant with thin or damaged credit can show ability to pay their share by other means. We say so in the criteria and we actually look at what they bring.
4. **The criteria are published and identical for everyone.** One document, posted with the listing, applied in the same order to every file. If an applicant ever asks why they were declined, the answer is in that document.
5. **Nobody says "we don't take Section 8."** Not in ads, not on the phone, not in a text. The phrase has no legal version.
6. **Every property is available to every qualified applicant.** We don't have voucher properties and non-voucher properties.
7. **We advertise where voucher holders actually look.** The settlement requires Wright Equities to do this going forward. It's easier to do it voluntarily.

## Rules change underneath you

I've been in California landlord-tenant work since 1978, and I've watched the ground shift under owners who were doing exactly what they'd always done. Source of income protection is one of those shifts. It's been the law for nearly seven years, and the Attorney General has now shown he's willing to spend investigators and expert witnesses to enforce it against a mid-sized property manager, not just a headline-sized one.

The lesson from Wright Equities isn't "stop setting lease terms." It's that any screening rule only a voucher holder can't satisfy is a liability, no matter how reasonable it sounds. If you self-manage, pull out your written criteria today and read them with that one test in mind. If you don't have written criteria, that's the first problem to fix.
