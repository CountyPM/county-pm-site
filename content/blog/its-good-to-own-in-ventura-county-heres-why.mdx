Every few months a chart goes around showing California at the bottom of the list for net domestic migration. The latest United States Census Bureau estimates put the state's loss at about 229,000 people in a single year, worst in the country by a wide margin. The chart gets shared with a caption like "Get out while you can."

I've owned and managed rental property in Ventura County since the 1970s, and I'd like to offer a different reading. The people leaving are not the story. The houses they leave behind, and the fact that nobody is building more of them, is the story.

## We decided, on purpose, not to grow

Ventura County has something almost no other metro area in America has: a voter-approved wall around every city. The SOAR initiatives (Save Open-space and Agricultural Resources) require a public vote before farmland or open space can be rezoned for development. Voters renewed them in 2016, and they run through 2050.

The practical effect is that Camarillo, Thousand Oaks, Ventura, Oxnard, Simi Valley and the rest cannot expand outward. Every new home has to come from infill: a lot split here, an accessory dwelling unit (ADU) there, a mid-rise on a former car lot. That kind of building is slow, expensive and fought over block by block. It adds units in the hundreds, not the thousands.

So when a household leaves for North Carolina, the house they sell is not replaced by a new one. It is simply absorbed. Supply does not respond to demand here, because we voted that it shouldn't.

## Santa Barbara already ran this experiment

If you want to know where this leads, drive an hour north. Santa Barbara drew its growth line decades ago, protected its agricultural belt, and let its population flatten while the rest of the state boomed. The result is a city where the teachers, nurses and hotel workers commute in from Ventura and Lompoc, where a modest house costs what a mansion costs elsewhere, and where rentals are scarce at any price.

Whatever you think of that outcome for a community, it has been very good to the people who owned property there and held on. Ventura County is the same model, about twenty-five years behind.

## The tax code pays you to stay

Here is the part that out-of-state commentators consistently miss. California does not just make housing scarce; it makes selling expensive and holding cheap.

Proposition 13 locks your property tax to what you paid, plus two percent a year. Sell and buy again, even in the same town, and your tax bill resets to today's value. If you refinanced in 2020 or 2021, you are sitting on a mortgage rate you will not see again in your lifetime. If the house has appreciated, and in this county it has, selling triggers capital gains tax at some of the highest combined federal and state rates in the country, plus recapture of every dollar of depreciation if it's a rental.

Hold it until you die, and the tax basis steps up to market value. The entire gain disappears for your heirs. The tax code's message to a Ventura County owner could not be clearer: the cheapest exit is no exit.

## Inflation does the rest

Add one more trend. The federal government carries a debt it has no realistic plan to repay except by inflating it away. In an inflationary decade, hard assets rise in nominal terms and fixed debts shrink in real terms. A landlord with a fixed-rate loan watches rent climb with the cost of living while the payment stays flat. Even under California's statewide rent cap, Assembly Bill 1482, allowable increases are tied to the Consumer Price Index (CPI), so rents follow inflation up by design.

Meanwhile, the family that sold here and moved to a fast-growing Sunbelt market holds equity in a place where supply can expand as fast as builders can pour slabs. Prices there can fall. Here, they mostly can't.

## What could go wrong

I'll be straight about the risks, because they are real. The same scarcity that protects owners creates political pressure, and California is the one state that might try to tax its way out of a problem it created: a split-roll challenge to Proposition 13, vacancy taxes, tighter rent control. Insurance costs are rising faster than rents in some areas. Water is a long-term question. And if the population decline turns from a trickle into something worse, no growth boundary saves you.

None of those is the base case. But an owner should know what would have to change for the story to break.

## The bottom line

The one thing this model doesn't fix is the work. Owning here is a good bet; managing here, with California's notice rules, deposit law and rent caps, is a job. Plenty of owners sell not because the asset stopped making sense but because the headaches did. That's a solvable problem, and it's a much smaller one than giving up a Proposition 13 basis and a four percent mortgage to escape it.

So the question for a Ventura County owner isn't "should I get out before everyone else does." It's whether you can carry what you have through the next vacancy, the next roof and the next insurance renewal. If you can, you own something that can't be rebuilt, in a place that decided not to build more, under a tax code that rewards you for staying put. Most of the people leaving will spend the next decade explaining why they sold.
