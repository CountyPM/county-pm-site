The phone call usually starts the same way: "One of us is moving out. What
do we need to do?"

After fifty years of managing rentals in Ventura County, I can tell you the
honest answer is more than anyone expects. When you approved that
application, you didn't approve three separate people. You approved a
household. When one person leaves, almost everything you decided at move-in
is back on the table.

Here's how I handle it, and what I'd want every owner to understand before
saying "sure, no problem."

## You qualified a household, not a person

When roommates apply together, their income, credit and rental history are
evaluated as a unit. Three incomes cover the rent comfortably; two might
not. One applicant's strong credit may have offset another's thin file.

So the first question when someone leaves isn't about paperwork. It's this:
**does the household that remains still qualify?** If it does, the path is
simple. If it doesn't, you have a decision to make, and it's better to make
it now than three months later when rent comes up short.

## Three ways forward

In practice, a roommate departure ends one of three ways:

1. **The remaining tenants re-qualify on their own.** They submit current
income and information, and if they meet your standards, you document the
change in writing.
2. **A replacement roommate applies.** The new person goes through the same
screening as any applicant. If approved, they're added to the rental
agreement and the departing tenant comes off, in writing, signed by
everyone.
3. **Everyone leaves.** If the remaining tenants can't qualify and there's
no acceptable replacement, the tenancy ends and you re-rent the home.

What doesn't work is the fourth option most people try by default: the
roommate moves out, someone new moves in, and nobody tells the landlord.
Now there's an unscreened occupant in your property who isn't on the
agreement, and a former tenant who may believe they're off the hook.

## The security deposit doesn't split itself

This is where most of the friction happens.

The security deposit is held for the tenancy as a whole, not in separate
envelopes for each roommate. Roommates often split the deposit unevenly at
move-in, or one person fronted it and the others paid them back. The
landlord rarely knows, and shouldn't have to.

My practice is straightforward: **I don't want to know who paid what.**
When a roommate leaves and a replacement comes in, the departing tenant's
share is settled between the roommates. Typically the incoming person pays
out the person leaving. The deposit stays in place for the continuing
tenancy.

When the tenancy finally ends, the refund and the itemized statement go to
all the tenants on the agreement. That keeps me out of disputes between
roommates, and it keeps the deposit accounting clean.

## Notice is where people get hurt

Here's the part most people get wrong, on both sides.

A roommate who sends a 30-day notice often believes that notice ends
*their* responsibility while everyone else stays. A landlord who receives
it may not be sure whether it ends the entire tenancy. On a joint rental
agreement, the answer depends on the agreement and the facts. It isn't
always what either side assumes, and it's worth getting advice from a
landlord-tenant attorney before you rely on one reading or the other.

The practical protection is the same either way. **Get it in writing,
signed by everyone affected, before anyone treats the move-out as final.**
That means:

- A written notice that names who is leaving and when.
- Written confirmation from the remaining tenants of whether they intend to
stay.
- A signed update to the rental agreement reflecting the new household.

Until that paperwork is done, treat the situation as unresolved.

## What owners should do the day they hear about it

- **Ask who is leaving, who is staying, and when.** Get the answer in
writing.
- **Re-check qualification** for whoever remains.
- **Require a full application** from any proposed replacement. No
exceptions for "a friend of ours."
- **Stay out of the deposit split** between roommates. Keep the deposit
tied to the tenancy.
- **Paper every change** with a signed amendment or new agreement.
- **If the whole household leaves,** follow your normal move-out process.
That includes the written notice of the tenants' right to a pre-move-out
inspection, which in California isn't optional.

## The bottom line

When a roommate moves out, three things change at once: who you're renting
to, who holds the deposit, and what the agreement says. Handled wrong, it
turns into miscommunication, hard feelings and a day in small claims court.
