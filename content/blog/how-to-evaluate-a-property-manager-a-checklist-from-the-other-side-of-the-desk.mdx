Most "best property manager" lists are written by property management companies that rank themselves first. This isn't one of those. I won't tell you whom to hire. I'll tell you what I would check if I were the owner sitting across the desk, after nearly 50 years on the manager's side of it.

Use this checklist in every interview, including one with us.

## Start with the license, not the website

**Look up the license before the first meeting.** Every California property manager collecting rent for others must work under a real estate broker licensed by the California Department of Real Estate (DRE). The DRE's public license lookup takes two minutes. Check three things:

- **Is the license active, and is there any disciplinary history?** Discipline records are public.
- **Who is the responsible broker?** In a corporation, one designated broker is legally accountable. Find out who that is.
- **Is that broker actually involved?** Some firms are run day to day by unlicensed staff under a broker who rarely sees the files. Ask directly how often the broker reviews your property's decisions.

## Ask where your money sits

**Your rent and the tenant's deposit are not the company's money.** California Business and Professions Code §10145 requires brokers to hold client funds in a trust account, separate from the company's operating money. Ask:

- **Which bank holds the trust account, and is it reconciled monthly?**
- **When does owner money go out each month, and how?** Get a specific date and method.
- **Who holds the security deposit, and in what account?**

A manager who is vague here is telling you something important.

## Read the management agreement for the exit, not the entrance

The most revealing part of any management agreement is how you get out of it. Look for:

- **The termination clause.** How much notice is required, and is there a cancellation fee?
- **What happens to the tenant if you leave.** Some agreements charge a fee if you keep a tenant the manager placed. Others claim the lease itself.
- **The full fee schedule.** Every charge you could pay in a year should be written down. I covered why in [What Does Property Management Really Cost in Ventura County?](https://www.c-p-m.com/blog/what-does-property-management-really-cost-in-ventura-county)

## Test how they choose tenants

**Ask for the written screening criteria, and ask who makes the final choice.** A professional manager applies the same written standards to every applicant and hands them to everyone before collecting a fee. Since January 1, 2025, California law (Assembly Bill 2493) requires every landlord to follow one of two paths. One is to approve the first qualified applicant in the order applications arrive. The other is to choose among applicants and refund the screening fee to everyone not selected.

Ask which path the manager uses. If the answer is "we send you the qualified files and you pick," the reason behind that choice lives in a phone call that was never written down, and that is where fair housing complaints start. A good manager has you approve the criteria before the property is marketed, not the applicant afterward. I explain why in [The Test Is Whether a Policy Exists](https://www.c-p-m.com/blog/the-test-is-whether-a-policy-exists).

## Ask to see the paperwork before you need it

You will never see most of a manager's work unless something goes wrong. So ask to see it now:

- **A sample move-in condition report**, with photographs.
- **A sample monthly owner statement**, so you know what you'll be reading every month.
- **The move-out process**, including the notice telling tenants they have a right to a pre-move-out inspection. California law requires it, and I learned that the hard way in [The Night Before](https://www.c-p-m.com/blog/the-night-before).

A manager who can't show you these documents in the interview won't have them in court.

## Find out how maintenance really works

- **What is the repair-approval limit?** Above what dollar amount will they call you first?
- **Do they mark up vendor invoices,** or own the maintenance company they send? Neither is automatically wrong, but you should know who benefits from each repair.
- **Who handles a 2 a.m. water leak,** and how fast?

## Ask about the bad year

Every tenancy can fail. Ask what happens when one does:

- **What happens if the tenant stops paying?** Walk through the timeline and who pays for what. Evictions in Ventura County commonly take four months or longer now.
- **How many evictions did they file last year, and how did they turn out?**
- **Who goes to court?** Is it the broker, a staff member, or an attorney billed to you?

## Count the doors

**Ask how many properties your individual manager handles.** A manager responsible for 300 doors cannot know yours the way one handling 80 can. Also ask who your single point of contact will be, and what happens when that person is on vacation.

## Check local knowledge

Ventura County is not one market. Oxnard has its own rent rules, the unincorporated county has others, and most cities follow only the statewide cap. **Ask the manager which rules apply to your specific address.** A good manager answers without looking it up.

## Talk to current owners, not just reviews

Online reviews skew toward tenants with complaints. **Ask for two or three current owner references,** ideally owners with properties like yours, and call them. Ask what went wrong during their time with the company, and how it was handled.

## Red flags

- **No written fee schedule,** or one that differs from the sales pitch.
- **Pressure to sign quickly.**
- **Guaranteed rent promises** with no explanation of how they are funded.
- **Vagueness about trust accounts** or when you get paid.
- **No sample documents** to show you.
- **A broker you never meet.**

## The bottom line

The right property manager is the one whose paperwork you've seen, whose fees you can add up, whose exit terms you can live with, and whose broker you've met. Interview at least two. Ask every question on this list. The answers, and how readily they're given, will make the choice for you.
