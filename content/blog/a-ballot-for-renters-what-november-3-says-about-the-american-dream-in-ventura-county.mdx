In the scorecard I published last week, Ventura County property owners went 5 for 19 on the measures and candidates that touched their interests. I said then that it wasn't a messaging problem. This is what I think it is.

## From a society of owners to a society of tenants

For most of the last century the American deal was simple: work, save, buy a house, and the house carries you. The postwar decades built an owning majority, and that majority set the rules. Proposition 13 passed in 1978 because enough Californians owned something it protected.

The last 15 years have been reversing that. According to the National Association of Realtors (NAR), first-time buyers are now 22% of home purchases, against a norm of 40% before 2008. The median first-time buyer is 40 years old; in the 1980s they were in their late 20s. The typical repeat buyer is 62. Delaying ownership a decade costs roughly $150,000 in lost equity, and that's the gentle version, because a growing share never catches up at all.

The attitudes follow the numbers. One in six aspiring buyers has given up on ever owning. Among Gen Z renters, 72% say renting is the smarter financial strategy. Two economists modeled that 15% of Americans born in the 1990s had already given up on buying by age 30, and found that once people give up, they save less, spend more, and take bigger risks. Most still say they want to own. They've stopped believing they will.

## Generational wealth versus the have-nots

Here's the part that should worry anyone who believes in ownership. The dividing line is no longer income. It's whether your parents owned.

A record 10% of first-time buyers used an inheritance to get in. First-time buyers paying all cash hit a record 10%, and their median age is 54: they're buying with a parent's house, not a paycheck. One in five first-time buyers lived at home before purchasing, up from one in eight in 1993, because skipping market rent is now the only way to assemble a down payment.

That's not a ladder. That's a gate, and it only opens for people whose family already walked through it. California is building two classes: households with equity, who pass it down, and households without, who pay rent to the first group indefinitely. An electorate split that way votes exactly the way Ventura County voted on November 3.

## Read the ballot that way and it makes sense

Every bond on real property passed. The two-thirds vote for initiative taxes failed. The justices who upheld a tax on $5 million sales were retained at 70%. To a voter who expects to rent for the next 15 years, every one of those was a tax on somebody else.

Now look at what renters protected. Proposition 42, banning new taxes on retirement accounts and personal property, passed. The 401(k) is the one asset a 35-year-old renter does expect to own. And the two candidates who campaigned on repealing Costa-Hawkins and the Ellis Act both lost, because the rental stock is where renters live. Voters protected what they hold. They didn't protect what they've stopped expecting to hold.

## Why this doesn't fix itself

Owners tend to assume the pendulum swings back. It won't on its own, for a reason that's purely arithmetic: the coalition that defended Prop. 13 for 48 years loses members every year the first-time buyer gets older, and gains none. Nobody is born into it; you have to buy your way in, and the door is narrowing. The Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) don't need to win the argument for social housing. They need to wait until nobody else is offering a path to ownership, and then social housing is the only path left.

## Ownership doesn't defend itself

Here is what I've concluded after 50 years in Ventura County real estate. The homebuilders have a lobby. The Realtors have a lobby. The large apartment owners have an association with a government-affairs staff. The owner of one rental, or four, or a dozen, has nobody. When a city council takes up a vacancy tax or an initiative drafter borrows the Los Angeles transfer-tax model, no one in the room speaks for the retired teacher who owns a duplex in Oxnard.

And no one, anywhere, makes the case for ownership to the 40-year-old renter who still wants in. The industry argues about rates and thresholds. It never argues that the renter should become an owner, and that the rules should make that possible.

That is the fight: not for landlords, for ownership. It needs four things that don't exist in this county today.

1. Someone watching every agenda item that touches rental housing before it reaches a vote, not after.
2. A professional in the room when it does.
3. Data on what the rules cost small owners, so the argument runs on numbers instead of anecdotes.
4. A message to renters that ownership is still the deal, and a willingness to back the policies that reopen the door, starting with more housing.

I'm working on what that looks like for the small independent owners of Ventura County. If you own property here and November 3 bothered you for the same reasons it bothered me, I'd like to hear from you now, before the next ballot is written. Use the contact form and put "ownership" in the subject line.

The American Dream isn't being repealed. It's being inherited. The question for everyone who got in is whether we hold the door or let it close behind us.
