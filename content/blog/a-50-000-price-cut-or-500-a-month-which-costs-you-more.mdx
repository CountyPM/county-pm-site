## The question every seller faces in a slowing market

Your home has been on the market for weeks. Your agent says the answer is a $50,000 price reduction. Or you could take it off the market, rent it out, and absorb about $500 a month in negative cash flow until conditions improve.

Most owners treat this as a gut decision. It shouldn't be. You can put numbers on it, and the numbers often surprise people.

## Why this question is coming up now

Mortgage rates have climbed back above 7%. The 30-year fixed averaged 7.28% in early October 2026, up from 6.34% a year earlier. The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate in September, and most officials expect at least one more increase.

Higher rates mean buyers qualify for less, some drop out entirely, and the ones who remain have time and leverage. Price reductions follow.

Rates won't stay where they are forever, but no one knows when they'll ease. That uncertainty is exactly why this choice matters.

## The simple math

A $50,000 price cut is permanent and immediate. Holding at $500 a month would take more than eight years to cost the same amount:

| Hold for | Cost at $500/month |
|---|---|
| 1 year | $6,000 |
| 2 years | $12,000 |
| 3 years | $18,000 |
| 8+ years | $50,000 |

On the surface, holding wins easily. But the real picture is better in some ways and riskier in others.

## Your $500 loss may really be about $350

A rental is a business, and its losses can be tax-deductible. Mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, repairs, management fees, and depreciation are all expenses. If you can deduct the loss against your other income, and your combined federal and California tax rate is around 30%, a $500 monthly loss costs you about $350 after tax.

| Hold for | After-tax cost (~$350/month) |
|---|---|
| 1 year | $4,200 |
| 2 years | $8,400 |
| 3 years | $12,600 |

One important limit: the rules for deducting rental losses against your wages or other income depend on your income. Many owners with active involvement can deduct up to $25,000 a year in rental losses, but that allowance phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income. Above that, the losses aren't lost. They carry forward and can be used when you sell. Your tax professional can tell you which group you're in.

## What the $500 is actually buying you

Negative cash flow isn't the whole story.

**Your tenant is paying down your loan.** Every payment includes principal. A few years into a typical California mortgage, the principal portion can run $1,000 or more a month. Your bank account goes down $500 while your equity goes up by more than that.

**You keep your low interest rate.** Many California owners hold loans at 3% or less. Once you sell, that rate is gone for good. Any buyer today pays more than 7% for the same house. That low-rate loan is an asset, and renting lets you keep it working for you.

**You choose when to sell.** This is the real value. You stop accepting whatever this month's buyers will pay.

## The catch: miss the window, lose the exclusion

This is where holding goes wrong for many owners.

If the home was your primary residence, you can generally exclude up to $250,000 of profit from tax, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. The rule is that you must have lived there two of the five years before the sale. In practice, **once you move out, you have about three years to sell.**

Miss that window and the exclusion is gone. On a home with $400,000 of profit, the combined federal and California tax could easily approach $100,000 for many owners. That is far more than the $50,000 price cut you were trying to avoid.

Two more details:

- **Depreciation comes back.** The depreciation you deduct while renting is taxed when you sell, even if the rest of the profit is excluded.
- **A 1031 exchange is a different path.** Once the home is truly an investment property, you may be able to defer the tax by exchanging into another investment property. It's a long-term strategy, not a way around the three-year deadline.

So the honest comparison for a former residence isn't "eight years of $500." It's **up to three years at roughly $12,600 to $18,000, with a hard deadline to sell.**

## $500 is the good month

Plan for the bad months, too. A vacancy, a water heater, a roof leak, or a slow-paying tenant can turn $500 into much more. Before you choose to hold, set aside a reserve: at least a few months of the full payment plus a repair cushion. If you can't fund a reserve, holding is riskier than it looks.

## The move-up safety net

This choice matters most to people buying their next home.

The usual move-up plan is to sell the current home, then buy the next one. In a slow market, that plan carries two risks: selling at a discount, or carrying two mortgages while the old home sits empty.

Renting the old home changes both:

- **Buy your next home on your schedule.** Many lenders will count part of the rent from a signed lease toward qualifying for your new loan. Ask your lender early.
- **The old house pays most of its own costs.** Instead of a full empty mortgage, you're covering a gap.
- **You get a three-year window to sell into a better market.** If rates ease and buyers return, you sell at a price you choose, with your exclusion intact.
- **If the market doesn't recover, you still sell on time.** You'll have spent some holding costs, but you won't have been forced into the worst moment.

Think of it as a safety net, not a permanent rental. The plan includes the exit from day one: a sale date inside the window and a lease term that ends before it.

## Set up the rental with the sale in mind

If you rent with plans to sell, the lease matters:

- **Match the lease term to your sale timing.** Don't sign a two-year lease if you need to list in 18 months.
- **Include California's exemption notice.** A single-family home owned by an individual can be exempt from the statewide rent cap and just-cause eviction rules, but only if the lease contains the required notice. Without it, wanting to sell is generally not a valid reason to end a tenancy.
- **Plan the handoff.** Selling a vacant, prepared home usually beats selling with a tenant in place, especially when buyers have choices.

## Which owner are you?

**Taking the price cut may be right if:**

- You need the cash now for your next purchase or retirement
- You can't fund a reserve for repairs and vacancy
- You're already near the end of your three-year window
- You don't want to be a landlord, even temporarily

**Holding may be right if:**

- The rent covers most of the payment
- You have a low-rate loan you'd hate to give up
- You have time left on your three-year window
- You have reserves and are willing to manage the property, or have it managed

## The bottom line

A $50,000 price cut is certain. Holding is cheaper on paper, but it's a bet on timing, and for a former home the bet has a deadline.
