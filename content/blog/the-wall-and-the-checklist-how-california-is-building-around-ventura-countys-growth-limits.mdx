## Are greedy landlords the reason housing costs so much?

No. High housing costs come from too many people competing for too few homes. Landlords are an easy target because they're visible. The real cause is simple math: if government policy boosts demand while limiting how much can be built, prices go up. Blaming the people who own existing housing does nothing to add more of it.

Nowhere shows this more clearly than Ventura County. In June 2026, the median price of an existing single-family home here was $937,500. The state says the county and its ten cities need to plan for roughly 24,000 new homes in the current 2021–2029 housing cycle. The gap between those two facts is a supply problem, not a landlord problem.

## What is SOAR, and how does it limit housing?

SOAR (Save Open Space and Agricultural Resources) is a set of voter-approved growth limits. Ventura County voters first passed it in 1998. Eight of the county's ten cities adopted their own versions; Ojai and Port Hueneme are the exceptions. In 2016, voters renewed all of them through 2050.

Each city's SOAR measure draws a line around it, called a CURB (City Urban Restriction Boundary). Building housing on farmland or open space outside that line requires a public vote.

SOAR has done what its supporters promised. Our farmland, greenbelts, and open hillsides are a big part of why people want to live here, and that is a real benefit. But every policy has a cost, and this one's cost falls on supply. When a city can't grow outward, the only places left to build are inside the line, and those parcels are scarce, expensive, and often fought over.

## Who pays for the wall?

The people who pay most are those trying to get in for the first time. Young couples who grew up here, teachers, nurses, and the children of longtime residents compete for a fixed stock of homes against buyers with decades of equity. Many rent longer than they planned. Others buy over the grade in Santa Clarita or farther out and commute back to jobs here.

A growing number skip the commute and leave California entirely, for Texas, Arizona, Nevada, Idaho, or wherever a paycheck still buys a house. When they go, Ventura County loses more than residents. It loses the next generation of workers, customers, and neighbors.

None of that is a landlord's doing. A landlord can't build a house on land voters have closed to building. Rents reflect scarcity; they don't create it.

## How has the state worked around local control?

Over the past decade, Sacramento has passed a series of laws aimed at one problem: local approval processes that let a single public hearing, appeal, or lawsuit kill a housing project. The state's answer was to change the question from "Does the council like this project?" to "Does this project meet the written rules?"

The planning term is *ministerial approval*. If a qualifying project meets a city's *objective standards*, meaning measurable rules like height, setbacks, and density that require no personal judgment, the city must approve it. There's no discretionary hearing and no "we'd prefer something else." In effect, the state handed cities a checklist.

- **SB 35 (2017), extended and expanded by SB 423 through 2036.** Qualifying multifamily projects in cities behind on their state housing goals get checklist approval on a fixed timeline.
- **SB 9 (2021).** Homeowners in most single-family zones can split a lot or build a duplex by right.
- **AB 130 (2025).** Qualifying infill housing that meets local zoning and objective design standards is exempt from CEQA (the California Environmental Quality Act), long the most common tool for delaying housing projects in court.

## What's the clearest example of the checklist at work?

Backyard and in-home units. A single-family lot can now hold an ADU (accessory dwelling unit), a JADU (junior accessory dwelling unit) built inside the house, and often more. Cities must approve them ministerially, with no hearing, on a 60-day clock.

Every one is built inside the urban boundary, on land that already has streets, sewer, and water. It is infill at the smallest scale, carried out by ordinary homeowners instead of developers.

## So do SOAR and state housing law conflict?

Less than you'd think. Put them side by side:

- **At the edge of our cities,** local voters hold the gate. Growth requires a public vote.
- **Inside the edge,** the state has largely taken the gate away. Growth requires passing a checklist.

Together they push nearly all new housing toward one place: infill within existing city boundaries. Vacant lots, underused commercial sites, aging strip centers, and backyards are now where Ventura County's future housing will be built. And the state has made it much harder for a city to say no.

## What does this mean for you?

- **First-time buyers:** most new supply will be townhomes, condos, apartments, and ADUs, not new single-family tracts. Your realistic first home may look different from your parents'.
- **Property owners:** if you own a lot in a single-family zone, you may have more options than you think. A second unit that would have died at a hearing ten years ago may now be a checklist item.
- **Investors weighing whether to stay in California:** the math has changed in both directions. See [Your Premise Was Right for Ten Years. It Just Inverted.](https://www.c-p-m.com/blog/your-premise-was-right-for-ten-years-it-just-inverted)
- **Everyone else:** when someone tells you the housing problem is greedy landlords, ask a simpler question. How many homes did we allow to be built last year, and where?
